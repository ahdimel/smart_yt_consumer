## What Mao actually was

Not a theorist of war between states — that's Clausewitz (bilateral land war), Sun Tzu (holding a
continental empire by coercion and deception), Mahan and Corbett (maritime power). Mao theorized
**triangle building**: an insurgent constructing passion, creativity and rationality — people,
military, government — *from nothing*, inside a host state, until the shadow government replaces
the real one. That is why his work exported so well to the decolonizing world: agricultural and
underdeveloped, like China and unlike industrial Russia.  `02:49`–`04:51`

Paine's framing, stated flatly: the military genius who reassembled a shattered country after 40
years of failure, and the psychopath incapable of running a peacetime economy. 40 million starved
in the Great Famine — the only nationwide famine in Chinese history, because it was caused by
policy rather than weather. Mao kept exporting grain through it; food exports were the
government's income. The dead were disproportionately rural peasant girls. (Figure from Yang
Jisheng, who mined provincial archives as a journalist; his father was among the dead.)
`04:51`–`08:54`

## The framework: Clausewitz's two indispensable qualities

*Coup d'œil* — the glimmer of inner light that finds truth in the darkest hour — and the
determination to follow it. Paine tracks four Maos: **propagandist, social scientist, operational
commander, grand strategist.**  `13:00`–`15:05`

### Propagandist
"The Communist Party can overthrow the enemy only by holding propaganda pamphlets in one hand and
bullets in the other." Propaganda was one of just six departments in the original shadow
government. Every military unit carried a **20-member propaganda team**; Mao called propaganda
work the Red Army's first-priority work — categorically unlike Western soldiering. Messengers
surfaced local grievances for the Party to fix, generating allegiance, while reporting back to
centre. Messages were kept epigrammatic — "Arm the peasants" — functionally tweets. He wrote
simple eight-character couplets for the semi-literate *and* difficult grass-writing for the
intellectuals he needed before he had educated peasants, then set poems to known tunes so they
spread by singing.  `15:05`–`23:20`

His international broadcast channel was foreign journalists, above all Edgar Snow.

### Social scientist
Between 1926 and 1933 Mao ran survey after survey, inventorying the countryside "down to the last
pitchfork and last chicken." Finding: **6% of the rural population owned 80% of the land.** He
segmented it — 70% poor peasants, 20% middle, 10% exploitative — and set out to mobilize the
bottom 80% to crush the top.

The sequencing was deliberate and duplicitous: **propagandize first, distribute land later.** Rich
peasants were preserved because "rich peasant production is indispensable" (a line cut from the
published collected works) until the civil war was won and the guns could turn. Middle peasants
got a bait-and-switch — land now, collectivization later. Class status was decided by local vote,
then routed through layers of party approval before returning to announce who received land and
who took a bullet.  `23:20`–`29:25`

### Operational commander
Mao's early career was **being right as a minority view** and being vindicated. Li Lisan tried to
seize cities on Moscow's instruction (Nanchang 1927, Changsha 1930) — both disasters; Mao's
diagnosis was that he "did not understand the protracted nature" of the war and sought a decisive
battle far too early. Xiang Ying was sent to fire Mao personally for ceding territory; the
resulting defend-everything strategy produced the Long March, in which the Communists lost 95% of
their forces. ("Decimate means lose 10%. Losing 95%, you need a whole new verb.")

Doctrine:
- **Base areas** on provincial boundaries where central authority doesn't reach. Four
  prerequisites: defensible terrain, Red Army presence, organized workers and peasants, strong
  party organization.
- **Six prerequisites to fight; you need at least two.** The most important is active popular
  support. Enemy weakness, exhaustion and error can appear fast — terrain cannot, so choose it
  carefully.
- **Annihilation, not attrition.** The weak destroy one small unit at a time and let the
  cumulative effect shift the balance. Only the strong can afford attrition.
- **Two services**: guerrilla and conventional. And a correction to the popular image — *"Regular
  forces are of primary importance because it is they who alone are capable of producing the
  decision. There is in guerrilla warfare no such thing as a war-winning battle."*
- **A terminal point of retreat**, chosen in advance. Mao picked Yan'an because it was near the
  Soviet border.
- **Disintegrate the enemy army**: insinuate workers and peasants into it as soldiers, porters and
  cooks. Paired with leniency — recruit willing POWs, release unwilling ones, so the contrast
  between Communist leniency and Nationalist brutality becomes stark. (Sun Tzu: never put your
  enemy on death ground.)

Allies no one had assembled before: peasants, **women** (half the population, badly treated,
therefore natural revolutionaries — he promised absolute equality and was decades ahead), youth,
intellectuals, minorities (offered self-determination — a promise, Paine notes, that became
unenforceable the moment it could be broken; ask the Tibetans and Uyghurs), and the enemy army.
`29:25`–`43:38`

### Three stages of people's war
Defensive → stalemate → offensive, and the activities are **cumulative, not sequential** — each
phase adds capability rather than replacing it. Two hard-won points:

The **phase-one-to-two transition is the dangerous one**: what looked like isolated banditry is
now legible as an insurgency bent on regime change, so the state changes strategy while you are
still weak. Westmoreland spent Vietnam trying to force exactly this concentration; the North
Vietnamese had read their Mao and declined.

**Phase three requires a big friend.** Conventional war needs an industrial base China lacked,
which is the real reason Yan'an was the retreat point — Mao was fighting his way toward Soviet
supply. This is the secret sauce of people's war.  `43:38`–`45:40`

### The claim that reframes the war
**Mao did not fight the war against Japan. The Nationalists did.** Every piece of conventional
fighting was theirs, bar Mao's Hundred Regiments campaign in 1940 — which drew the Japanese
"three alls" reprisal (kill all, burn all, loot all), destroyed base areas across North China, was
never repeated, and never appeared in his collected works. While the Nationalists were destroyed
fighting Japan, the Communists mobilized the countryside and later took the credit.  `47:46`

## The interview

**Why the shrewd commander produced the Great Leap Forward** `1:02:14` — Paine rejects the
premise that welfare was ever the objective: staying in power was. Beyond that, the underlying
theory was simply wrong (labour theories of value don't account for services), and no individual
has expertise in everything — Churchill won the war and was voted out immediately after. Communes
weren't only ideological; they were the mechanism for extending central control over a shattered
continental population by controlling who eats.

**Mao vs. Stalin** `1:04:14` — the Holodomor was *intentional*; the Great Leap was delusion.
Russia had been industrializing since the late 19th century and had institutions China lacked.
Compare the wreckage: Taiping (20–30M dead), decades of peasant rebellions, warlords, the
Communist-Nationalist war, then Japan. Her calibration: the US Civil War was shorter, less
brutal, left Washington's institutions intact and let Lee's army go home — *and the losing side
still hasn't gotten over it.*

**Why no successful coup** `1:26:43` — the commissar system is superb at seizing and holding
power and terrible at producing prosperity. Liu Shaoqi and Peng Dehuai did pull the plug on Mao;
the Cultural Revolution was his counterattack, launched after Khrushchev's 1956 denunciation of
Stalin showed him what happens posthumously. He left Beijing, rallied teenagers who began by
killing their teachers, then used Lin Biao to restore order once his rivals were gone.

**On Edgar Snow** `1:41:11` — Mao wanted his own John Reed and picked a young, naive, genuinely
talented writer. Snow never asked why an A-list leader was giving him hours. Mao corrected the
interview drafts. Across successive editions of *Red Star Over China*, purged figures vanish from
the photographs, the text and the index — and Snow went along, because the book was the only
thing that made him important. He denied the famine to the end.

**The Marshall Plan counterfactual** `1:14:28`–`1:22:39` — the disagreement Dwarkesh presses
hardest and loses. The US gave Europe $13bn and China roughly one-hundredth of that while
embargoing Chiang in 1946. Paine's answer: distinguish nation-*re*building from nation-building.
Germany and Japan already had institutions, literacy, courts and police, so cash rebuilt them
fast. China had none of that, so a Marshall Plan would not have worked — the same error later
repeated in Iraq and Afghanistan. The China hands, missionaries' children fluent in Chinese, had
correctly assessed it as hopeless, and were destroyed in the McCarthy purges for being right.

Her broader lesson, stated as a rule: **when two parties are primary adversaries, you cannot make
them cooperate.** Aid to one enrages the other. That's why the Nationalist-Communist coalition
government was always a non-starter, and it generalizes to India/Pakistan and to Iraq.

**Taiwan as rebuke** `1:34:58` — after losing, the KMT ran an after-action report, concluded
corruption and the absence of land reform had killed them, and did on Taiwan what they couldn't do
on the mainland, since there the landlords *were* the officer corps. Redistribution was bloody;
Taiwanese landowners were paid in bonds they assumed were worthless and which became valuable. By
~1990 Taiwan held 1/50th the population and 1/250th the land of the mainland at ~40% of its GNP —
and it retired the notion that Han culture and democracy are incompatible.

**On Xi** `1:30:49` — reveres Mao; educated during the Cultural Revolution, when institutions
weren't delivering education. "Don't ever kid yourself that the Communist Party of China doesn't
believe in communism now."
