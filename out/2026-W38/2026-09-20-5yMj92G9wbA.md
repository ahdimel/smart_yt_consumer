## The claim that carries the video

AI capex commitments are real, contractual, and largely invisible where investors look for them.
Not hidden — *disclosed*, in 10-Q footnotes — but off the balance sheet, and therefore absent
from Google Finance, from brokerage apps, and from the leverage and lease ratios screeners
compute (`03:20`). The Wall Street Journal totals them at roughly $3 trillion: backstops,
uncommenced leases, purchase agreements.

Meta's Hyperion campus in Louisiana is the clean example (`03:55`). Neither the facility nor its
$27bn of debt sits on Meta's balance sheet — Blue Owl Capital holds the debt, Meta is partner and
tenant. Nothing fraudulent, but a screener reading Meta's leverage returns a number that excludes
it. Many such items are 25-year leases, payable whether or not the AI revenue arrives (`04:30`).

The video is fair about its own number: Alphabet's $811bn of commitments is mostly long-dated
leases, not cash leaving next quarter (`05:06`). The guarantees are the concerning part. Nvidia
has committed $15bn backing OpenAI's financing — three times Nvidia's entire publicly disclosed
debt (`05:20`).

## The circularity, drawn properly

In August 2026 Nvidia partnered with six investment and PE firms targeting over $500bn for AI
buildings and equipment — a fundraising target, not cash in hand (`08:00`). The structure it
enables: outside lenders fund a data-center project, the project buys Nvidia systems, an AI
company promises to rent the compute. Nvidia books a chip sale today against rent earned over
years.

The Ohio deal is the worked example (`09:14`). SB Energy builds the campus; OpenAI rents space and
power; lenders fund construction against expected rent; Nvidia backstops OpenAI's rent and power
up to $105bn across nine sites, and in exchange OpenAI commits to Nvidia chips only. SoftBank owns
both SB Energy and a large stake in OpenAI. Each contract is collateralized by the next one.

The load-bearing point follows directly (`10:18`): the only money entering this system from
outside comes from OpenAI or from end users, and OpenAI is unprofitable for the foreseeable
future. The structure requires lenders to keep funding it long enough to reach profitability.
Related signal — banks that lent against an Oracle data center are offloading those loans to
private credit (`10:40`), moving the exposure somewhere less liquid and less visible.

Burry's summary: debt-funded capex puts the boom on a countdown, because interest accrues and
principal comes due. He dates the break to 2027, certainly before 2028 (`10:50`).

## Three warning signs, which is the usable part

Discard the crash call and a falsifiable residue remains (`12:20`). The most exposed players are
the neoclouds — CoreWeave, Nebius — already paying hundreds of millions in interest and holding
far less leverage than Google or Nvidia if contracts need renegotiating (`11:50`). They need two
things at once: customers signing ever-larger contracts, and lenders extending ever-larger loans.
Watch for **excess capacity** (compute contracted but not consumed), **lender fatigue** (failure
to refinance), and **rate shocks**. All three are observable now, unlike the 2027 date.

## Contagion

AI stocks are close to a third of the S&P 500, so this is not a sector trade for a passive holder
(`13:26`), and Burry's 2022 argument compounds it: passive flows mechanically weight the largest
names, propping up whatever bubble exists.

The margin figures are the video's other new data (`14:26`). US customer margin debt hit a record
$1.5tn in June 2026, up ~38% year over year; Robinhood's margin loans passed $21bn in Q2, up 127%.
Where that money went is unknown, but forced selling into a decline is the feedback loop that
separates a crash from a correction.

## The advice, and where it's shakier than it sounds

Three items, all sensible, none novel (`15:30`): pay down margin and high-interest debt; check
allocations, since an index fund *plus* individual megacaps double-counts the same exposure; don't
panic sell. The host adds that he would never short — Burry nearly went bankrupt waiting three
years from his 2005 housing call to the 2008 payoff (`14:55`). That caveat is the honest part most
Burry videos omit.

Two soft spots. The Barclays call that the Fed will *raise* rates for two straight quarters
(`11:00`) is asserted, not sourced, and it does real work — it's what pulls the timeline earlier.
And "over a 20-year period the market has never had a negative return" (`16:34`) holds only for
rolling windows and only for the US.

None of the accounting analysis depends on Burry. The WSJ did the reporting, the deal structures
are public, and his contribution is a quote and a name in the title — one the video's own
track-record chart concedes has been wrong repeatedly (`00:52`).
