## The mechanism

A bond is a loan. The only difference is that it can be sold on: if Jack owes Gary £100, Gary can
sell that IOU to Dave for £90 and now Jack owes Dave (`02:00`). Governments cover most spending
from tax and borrow for crises and big capital projects, and they have borrowed steadily more
through 2008 and COVID.

UK government debt is a little over 100% of GDP, about £43,000 per person — per person, not per
adult (`04:01`). That is unremarkable for the West: the US, France and Italy owe 120–130%, Germany
and Australia 50–60%, Japan around 200%.

The load-bearing point is the refinancing ratchet (`04:40`–`12:03`). The average gilt runs 12–13
years, and because the government has been borrowing for centuries, a big redemption falls due
roughly every three months — 22 July, 22 October, 29 January, 7 March 2027. It never repays these;
it refinances, auctioning new debt to traders. Between 2008 and 2022 base rates were at or near
zero and the government borrowed at 1% or less. Those loans are now maturing into a market where
ten-year gilts yield 4.77%. So the average rate paid on the entire stock creeps upward with every
auction, mechanically, without any new policy decision.

Whether that matters is one subtraction. Inflation (I) and real growth (G) shrink debt relative to
the economy, because the debt is nominal and mostly not index-linked; the interest rate (R) grows
it. UK trend growth is about 1% and the inflation target is 2%, so I+G ≈ 3% against R = 4.77%. The
gap is 1.77% a year of self-propelled debt growth (`11:00`).

From there it compounds, and then it compounds faster than compounding, because traders watch.
Rising debt raises doubt about repayment — or raises the suspicion that the government will
inflate the debt away, which is worse for a creditor than a haircut — which raises the rate
demanded, which worsens the finances (`12:03`). That is the doom loop.

It has already fired twice. Liz Truss was removed in roughly 45 days in 2022. In January 2025 gilt
yields climbed far enough that, six months into the Starmer government, the same thing was live;
Labour responded by shredding its spending plans and hunting for benefit cuts, and was rescued by
a good inflation print (`14:04`). Gary's claim from talking to Labour politicians is that
everything since has been governed by fear of a repeat (`16:05`), and that every other Western
government is on the same path.

## What bondholders actually want

Why buy a bond at all when stocks and gold have run? Diversification and security (`20:05`).
Bonds have no upside — you get your money back plus 4–5% — but they hold their value in exactly
the crises that take 20–30% off equities, which is why the wealthy hold a slice, and why pension
funds, insurers and banks sitting on deposits hold a lot.

That produces three preferences (`24:22`), and they explain nearly every headline:

1. **Low inflation.** Inflation lifts the price of stocks, gold, land and houses, but not a fixed
   bond payout. Bondholders are the group inflation actually robs.
2. **Stable government finances.** "The return *of* your capital is more important than the return
   *on* your capital." Two risks: outright inability to pay, and the money printer.
3. **Low central bank rates.** If you think cash will pay 10% next year, you will not buy a 5%
   bond today.

## The Burnham test

The best-argued minutes in the video (`29:45`–`34:45`). The Iran war pushes oil, oil is in the
price of everything, so inflation is uncertain, so the central bank path is uncertain — and all
three things bondholders care about therefore hinge on a war of unknown length. Gilts have been
abnormally volatile as a result: moves that used to take a month arrive in a day.

That has coincided with the Starmer-resignation drama, so the press — the FT included — has been
attributing gilt moves to Andy Burnham. Gary's test is simply to open Bloomberg and look at
Germany, France, Italy, Spain, the US, Japan and Australia on the same day. They all move 0.15–0.2%
together. A synchronised global move is not caused by a British mayor. This is the one moment in
the hour where he shows his work rather than asserting.

## The thesis the video exists to deliver

Two further misconceptions, and the second is the real payload.

The left's version — *ignore the bond markets* — fails on one graph (`34:45`). Government wealth
as a share of GDP ran around +100% from the 1950s to the early 1980s and is now around −100%: from
owning things and owing little, to owning nothing and owing a lot. Since Thatcher and Reagan,
governments have sold the productive assets that funded public services and replaced them with
borrowing. This is not a left-right split; Biden and Trump both expanded borrowing.

Hence the conclusion (`38:10`): **you can tax the rich or borrow from the rich, and if you have
spent forty years choosing the second, you do not then get to dismiss the bond markets.** Being
dominated by three hundred traders in the City is not a separate problem from wealth inequality;
it is the same fact stated twice.

The third misconception is that a wealth tax would be punished by the markets (`40:10`). Bond
markets are not the disembodied spirit of Margaret Thatcher — they want high taxes and low
spending, because they want repayment. Capacity to tax the very rich is, on its own terms,
creditor-friendly. (The aside about economists who loathe wealth taxes while liking inheritance
taxes, which from the holder's perspective are the same tax, lands.)

The conceptual frame (`42:10`–`48:00`): wealth is ownership of real things — land, buildings,
resources — and debt is a claim layered on top. Post-war governments could provide health,
education and housing because they owned the assets that produce them and owed little. Now
ordinary people neither own the assets nor are free of debt to those who do, so they pay twice:
for access, and in interest.

## Three options

The UK has the highest borrowing costs of any major Western nation and is, he argues, the first
Western government to hit the wall — you can only sell the family silver once, and creditors stop
lending eventually (`51:00`). What remains is three choices and no fourth: tax the very rich much
more, tax working people (including high earners) much more, or continue dismantling the welfare
state. He guarantees every Western country does at least one, probably two, within ten years
(`52:15`). Remove option one politically and what is left is a fight between the poor and the
middle — after which the concentration continues anyway (`54:16`). The closing political
prediction (`57:20`): if Burnham fails on living standards, Farage; Farage also fails; and the
1930s pattern is that each failure ratchets further right.

## Where the confidence outruns the evidence

Three gaps worth holding.

The r−g arithmetic at `11:00` compares today's *market* yield to trend growth plus target
inflation, but the rate actually paid on the outstanding stock is much lower and converges only
over the 12–13 year maturity he himself describes. 1.77% is a steady-state endpoint, not the
current burden. More importantly, r−g governs the debt path *only given the primary balance* — a
primary surplus offsets it entirely. The primary balance is never mentioned in an hour about debt
sustainability, and that is a real hole, not a quibble.

The Burnham refutation is good but incomplete. Global co-movement rules out Burnham as the cause
of the *level*; it does not rule out a UK-specific spread widening riding on top. The clean test
is gilts against Bunds, not gilt yields in isolation.

And the wealth-tax argument answers the wrong objection. Nobody serious claims bondholders
ideologically hate wealth taxes; the claim is that they price in capital flight and collection
failure. Gary calls people who disagree "a little bit dumb" rather than addressing behavioural
response, which is where the actual argument is.

He also says "Andy Haldane" twice at `34:09` when he means Burnham.

Two substantive items. One commenter (27) corrects the country comparison at `04:01`: Japan holds

sets the rate and authorises spending by bill, the doom loop is not a constraint but a policy

PFI as the same refinancing trap applied to hospitals (15), an unanswered question about taxing
