A rock-and-ice avalanche off Langtang Lirung on 26 August 2026 killed on the order of thousands of
people in Nepal in under an hour, and the video's real argument is not about the flood — it's that
the disaster was invisible to every monitoring system pointed at the Himalayas, and that seven
minutes is less time than any warning chain can use.

## What actually happened

At 8:37 a.m. a block of bedrock on the remote northern face of Langtang Lirung (7,227 m, 99th
highest on Earth), at roughly 5,200 m elevation, failed all at once and took the glacial ice it had
been supporting with it — `03:00`. Hundreds of millions of tons, which RLL sizes at about ten New
York City blocks of material, fell more than 2,000 vertical metres into the Lhende river valley —
`04:01`. The impact released energy the video puts near that of the Hiroshima bomb and generated
seismic waves read worldwide as a magnitude-5.2 event. USGS initially logged an earthquake before
the unusual signature was recognised for what it was: not a quake that collapsed a mountain, but a
mountain collapsing hard enough to look like one — `05:02`.

Frictional heating pulverised the ice into slush and water, which mixed with the rock, sediment and
river already in the valley. Early reporting described a temporary landslide dam that later burst;
RLL says the emerging consensus rejects that — damming was limited and the flow was essentially
continuous from the moment the avalanche hit the valley floor — `06:04`. The mass ran up the far
valley wall by as much as 450 m, turned west, and accelerated down one of the steepest and
narrowest river valleys on the planet at roughly 50 m/s, about 110 mph — `07:06`.

## The seven minutes

This is the strongest section and the reason the video exists. The Rasuwagadhi–Gyirong crossing sat
20 km downstream and was hit at 8:44 a.m. The crossing had opened at 8:00; by 8:20 there were an
estimated 300–400 people waiting to leave for Tibet, plus arrivals coming the other way. Nepal's
electronic immigration system recorded its last transaction at 8:35 — two minutes before the
collapse. 89 people had cleared, and clearing immigration is followed by a 15-to-20-minute walk
down to the bridge, meaning some of them were walking directly toward the flow while it was already
moving — `09:08`.

Downstream instrumentation tells the same story from the other side: the hydrological station at
Syabrubesi last reported at 8:40 with the river at a perfectly normal 1.62 m, and stopped
transmitting at 8:50 — `12:11`. Nepal's flood forecasting division was receiving reports by around
9:00; emergency SMS warnings reached the ~680,000 people living along downstream riverbanks at 9:15
and 9:16, about forty minutes after the collapse. The communities nearest the source were already
gone. The conclusion RLL draws is not that the warning system failed but that no cross-border
warning system produces an evacuation of a deep gorge in seven minutes.

## Downstream damage

19 bridges and roughly 40 km of highway damaged or destroyed; Timure's dry port and much of the
111 MW Rasuwagadhi hydroelectric project, commissioned in 2024 after nearly a decade of
construction, erased — `11:09`. At Gauchi, more than 100 km from the origin, the river still rose
as much as 9 m within thirty minutes of the surge arriving, after the material had descended more
than 4,500 vertical metres — `13:12`. A one-metre flood pulse was detectable several hundred
kilometres downstream near the Indian border, and bodies were recovered nearly to the plains.

Toll as of production: 1,300+ confirmed dead in Nepal and 5,000+ missing, including around 600
foreign nationals; 16 dead and 540+ missing on the Tibetan side. A final figure near 7,000 is
plausible, which would make it the deadliest flood in Nepal's recorded history.

## The economic argument

Around $5 billion in damage, about one-tenth of GDP, in a country still paying down the 2015
earthquake ($9bn) and now spending roughly 19% of government outlays on debt service, after a
decade-long civil war and the Gen Z uprising that toppled the government in 2025 — `16:14`.

The sharpest point here is the hydropower trap. Nepal's main strategy for weaning itself off
remittances is its ~43 GW of commercially exploitable hydro potential, exportable into India. The
best sites are precisely the steep Himalayan valleys most exposed to events like this. The flood
damaged or destroyed 14 hydroelectric projects and knocked out about 12% of national generating
capacity, with hundreds of workers missing, some trapped in underground tunnel networks — `17:15`.
The infrastructure built for resilience was among the largest single losses. Separately, the
destroyed crossing carried roughly a third of Nepal's ~$3bn annual trade with China and about 80%
of its EV imports, at 60–70 heavy trucks a day — `18:16`.

## Why nobody was watching

No rain, no preceding earthquake, and no established mechanism — researchers still don't know why
this slope failed when it did. The general claim is about background conditions: high-altitude
permafrost frozen into bedrock cracks acts as glue, glaciers physically buttress steep rock walls,
and as high elevations warm faster than elsewhere both supports weaken — `20:17`. RLL is explicit
that this does not attribute the Langtang Lirung collapse to climate change.

The monitoring argument is the part worth keeping. Nepal's warning network is built for the hazards
it already knows: monsoon flooding and glacial lake outburst floods. It had even been rehearsed —
in July 2025 a supraglacial lake drained into the same river, destroyed the same bridge at the same
crossing and killed 19 people, after which lake monitoring in the region increased — `23:20`. The
2026 collapse came from an ordinary-looking slope nobody had flagged, sitting in a narrow sliver of
Nepalese territory that drains *north* into Tibet, in the gap between Nepalese monitoring focused
on the southern side of the range and Chinese monitoring focused on lakes. Of roughly a dozen major
Himalayan collapse events in the past decade, only one — the 2023 South Lhonak outburst in Sikkim —
occurred somewhere researchers had already identified as a serious risk — `25:22`.

Three recent analogues make the case that this is not a Himalayan problem: a 2023 rock-ice avalanche
into an east Greenland fjord that generated ~200 m waves; the May 2025 Birch Glacier collapse that
buried Blatten in Switzerland, where warning signs *were* read and the village was evacuated in
time; and August 2025 at Tracy Arm, Alaska, where over 2 billion cubic feet of rock produced a
1,580-ft runup — the second-highest tsunami ever recorded — in a popular cruise fjord that happened
to be completely empty at 5:26 a.m. Cruise ships had been there the day before — `22:19`.

## What the comments contest

**The energy figure is badly understated.** A well-upvoted technical comment notes that landslides
convert only 0.001–0.1% of their energy into seismic waves; working backwards from the magnitude
gives ~1.34 megatons TNT as a *conservative floor*, i.e. around 80× Hiroshima rather than roughly
one. Another commenter independently lands at ~79 Hiroshimas for the initial 1.2 km drop. If
correct, the video's most quotable comparison is off by two orders of magnitude.

**The footage understates the wave.** A commenter claiming local knowledge argues most clips shown
are from ~80 km downstream, where the flow looks survivable; within 30 km of the source the wave
exceeded 100 m, and at Timure and Syabrubesi it ran 60–90 m — neither of which is shown. They also
say the river looks low downstream because 15–30 m of deposited material raised the bed, and that
the initial flow was ~80% rock and mud, not water. Boulders were perched 60 m up.

**The numbers are already stale, and one claim cuts against the thesis.** A comment dated 18
September 2026 gives 1,600+ confirmed and 6,000+ missing, and asserts the mountain "had indications
of potential trouble weeks before the disaster, but no one took heed." That directly contradicts
the video's central framing that nothing distinguished this slope beforehand. It's unsourced and
the single claim here most worth verifying before repeating.

**The worst case didn't happen.** Several commenters point out the Lhende feeds the Gandaki and
thence the Ganges; this event stayed in the hills. One argues the real exposure is a comparable
flow that reaches the plains of southern Nepal, Uttar Pradesh and Bihar. Another notes the
dam-overtopping version of this risk applies to glacier-fed reservoirs in the Alps and North
America — `16:50`.

**Script nits, loudly.** "Thousands of years from now, our ancestors might wonder…" is the top
comment on the video at 2,100 likes; the repeated "8:37 a.m. in the morning" draws several more.
Multiple commenters thank RLL for dropping the background music, and one wishes the video had
covered the school principal who evacuated his students in time — the one piece of good news in the
event, and it's absent.
