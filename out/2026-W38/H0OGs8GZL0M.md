The Fed raised a quarter point — its first hike since 2023 — and Felix Prehn's argument is that it
is the wrong tool aimed at the wrong disease. Oil is above $100, diesel is at $7/gal, and fertilizer
and equipment costs are up 40–140% on the year. That is supply-side inflation: prices are rising
because inputs got more expensive to produce, not because demand is overheating. Rate hikes cool
demand. They do nothing to the Houthis, to fertilizer feedstock, or to the price of diesel. So the
hike leaves the cause untouched and lands entirely on the borrower — the mortgage, the small
business loan, the farmer financing next season. "The medicine doesn't treat the disease, it just
kills the patient."

That case runs from `03:03` to about `09:06` and it is the honest part of the video. The
pass-through mechanism is laid out cleanly: farmer pays more for diesel, charges more for grain;
haulier pays more for diesel, charges more for transport; shop pays more for delivery; the loaf
costs more. An energy shock does not stay in the energy aisle. The 1970s precedent is the payoff —
stagflation, a term economists had said couldn't describe a real state, and gold running from ~$100
to $850.

The second load-bearing stretch is `12:11`–`16:15`, on why the Fed is trapped rather than stupid.
Citing the *Financial Times*: $8 trillion of Treasuries roll over in the next twelve months, issued
at an average 3.3% and refinanced near 5%, adding roughly $130bn a year in interest. Every notch
higher on rates raises the price of the government's own mortgage. The Volcker comparison is the
sharp end of it — Volcker could hold double-digit rates because federal debt had fallen to about 30%
of the economy; at ~120% today the same move bankrupts the issuer first. Conclusion: they can hike a
quarter point and look tough, but they cannot hold rates high long enough to actually kill
inflation. So the debt gets inflated away instead, and the holder of dollars pays for it.

Two supporting pieces: $457bn of government debt has to find a buyer within four days of recording
(`17:15`), and if private demand falls short the Fed becomes the buyer — the 1940s move, and
inflationary by design. And central bank gold buying is running at its fastest pace since around
1997 (`20:18`). The chart at `09:06` is the strongest single exhibit: since 2000, dollar
purchasing power down, median US home $160k → ~$450k, gold $200 → near $5,000.

Everything else is scaffolding for the sell. The video is gated front and back by inflationtrap.com,
a free two-hour live training, pitched at `02:02`, again at `10:10`–`12:20`, and again in the close
at `23:18`. There is a free research report at `02:02`, and the Winston Metals & Stock app gets
three separate reads (`16:45`, `19:30`, `22:00`). Roughly six and a half minutes of the
twenty-five are direct promotion. Note the claim at `01:00`: "We don't take any sponsorship here. We
never affiliated anything. There's no link to get a brokerage account." True as stated, and
misleading — the monetisation is first-party rather than absent, and the video sells a founders-tier
app subscription and a webinar funnel.

Where the reasoning wobbles: the thesis at `15:15` is that they cannot hold rates high, followed at
`22:18` by "they're going to raise rates" — the sequencing never resolves. The fertilizer claim at
`07:06` ("half the world's fertilizer comes out of Iraq") is wrong and appears to be a garble of
something about sulfur. The $457bn at `17:15` becomes "450 billion" by `22:18`. And the 1971 dollar
being "worth about 7 cents… I think that's a highly inflated number, it's worth a fraction of a
cent" is rhetoric, not arithmetic. The actual portfolio advice, when it finally arrives at `21:18`,
is unremarkable and reasonable: gold, some silver, don't panic-sell good companies, and check
whether your "diversified" index holdings are really a 70% bet on AI.

## What the comments contest

The fertilizer error got caught and corrected politely by a viewer who went and checked — "Iraq does
not provide half of the world's fertilizers." Nobody disputed the correction.

The sharpest challenge is to the causation, not the conclusion: "Don't be daft. The Fed raised
because the world is not buying bonds or investing in dollars — not because of inflation." That
reading actually fits Prehn's own `17:15` auction segment better than his inflation-theater framing
does, and he never addresses it. A second commenter tightens his best number rather than disputing
it: refinancing 3.3% debt at 5% is close to a 50% increase in interest payments, which is a more
vivid statement of the same fact.

The track record is where the audience pushes back hardest. "Can you please review all the 'resets'
you have posted about so far and clarify how many of them actually happened" and "this clown has
been making these predictions for four years now, check his YouTube video titles." The defence
offered is not that the predictions landed but that the trade did anyway: "in that 4 years, gold has
more than doubled."

One reader rejects the framing of who gets hurt: the hike "only affects institutional borrowers,"
not the farmer with a tractor loan, and the institutions respond with buybacks — which would gut the
pass-through story at `07:06`. Another flatly denies the premise: "There is no oil shortage. There
is a CONTROLLED oil stoppage" (18 likes, no engagement from the channel).

And the questions the video creates but doesn't answer are the top-voted practical ones: gold and
silver *miners* (16 likes), what a European holder should do if the whole analysis is dollar-denominated
(5 likes), and whether ETC wrappers like SGLN/SSLN carry the counterparty risk the thesis implies.
All three point at the gap between the argument and an actual allocation — which is, of course,
exactly what the two-hour webinar is selling.
