## The argument

Money's one unique property is that it moves economic value through time (`01:05`). Everything else
about it is instrumental, so the planning question is not how much you can accumulate but what it
is for.

Seligman's decomposition: happiness = biological set point + circumstances + voluntary activities
(`01:40`). The financially load-bearing part is that circumstances — where you live, health, age,
education, gender — explain surprisingly little, because humans adapt fast, while voluntary
activities, how you allocate time and money, explain a lot (`02:01`). The exceptions are things
adaptation doesn't neutralize: intermittent noise, commuting in traffic, loss of control (or the
feeling of it), and distance from nature (`02:30`–`03:10`).

Two kinds of happiness — hedonia (feeling good) and eudaimonia (flourishing, measured by the
Cantril ladder) — neither sufficient alone (`03:10`). Seligman's PERMA model breaks wellbeing into
positive emotion, engagement/flow, relationships, meaning, and accomplishment, each pursued for its
own sake (`04:10`). Via Haidt's elephant (system 1) and rider (system 2), the elephant wants
pleasure and social rank; four of the five PERMA factors require the rider to be consciously engaged
(`04:40`). So letting system 1 set financial priorities buys experienced happiness at the expense of
reflective happiness — the same mechanism behind overeating and concentrated stock picking (`05:30`).

## The numbers

Jebb et al. 2018, 1.7M people (`06:03`). Income satiation for experienced happiness: $60k globally,
$65k North America. Experienced *un*happiness stops falling at $75k / $95k. Life evaluation satiates
at $95k / $105k. Past that, in North America, life evaluation *declines* with income; the authors
speculate high incomes carry time demands that crowd out leisure (`07:03`).

## Time, not money, is the binding constraint

Whillans (`08:05`): people who value time over money are happier, more socially connected, and more
satisfied with their jobs, and valuing time is prosocial — time affluence makes you more available to
others, which feeds back into your own happiness. Time poverty is largely self-inflicted by the
pursuit of money, because people believe more money today buys future happiness and systematically
undervalue their own time. The prescription runs the trade backward: buy time with money by
outsourcing unpleasant tasks (`09:06`).

Dunn, Gilbert and Wilson 2010 (`09:40`): spend on experiences over things (harder to adapt to,
better remembered, more often shared), on many small frequent pleasures over few large ones, and on
other people. The good experiences are mostly not the expensive ones.

## Why goal-based financial planning is the wrong shape

Two failures (`12:08`). The progress principle: pleasure comes from movement toward a goal, and
post-goal pleasure is short-lived. And the end-of-history illusion (Quoidbach, Gilbert, Wilson):
people believe they have just become who they will remain, despite having visibly changed. A plan
anchored to a 30-year-out retirement vision is a plan built for someone who won't exist.

The inversion is anti-goals (`13:08`): instead of sacrificing the present for a forecast future,
remove the parts of your present life you least want. Test each major financial decision against the
five PERMA factors, now and later.

The implication with real planning consequence is at `14:50`: eliminating work — normally the point
of the whole exercise — may be the wrong target. Haidt's claim is that love and work are the two
things that pull people out of themselves, and planning to work longer at a job you actually like
materially changes the savings rate and portfolio risk required to fund independence.

## Where the confidence exceeds the evidence

The opening fMRI hook (`00:10`) is reverse inference — shared activation with drug reward is not
evidence of addiction. More importantly, the satiation result was already contested when this
published: Killingsworth's PNAS paper from January 2021 found experienced wellbeing still rising past
$75k with no satiation point, and the 2023 Killingsworth–Kahneman–Mellers adversarial collaboration
split the difference (satiation for an unhappy minority, continued rise for everyone else). The video
presents the dollar figures as settled. Seligman's "equation" is a framing device, not a measurement,
and both experiences-over-things and prosocial spending sit in replication-crisis territory.

The thesis survives all of that — money's contribution to wellbeing saturates well below the top of
the income distribution — but the specific numbers are softer than they sound.
