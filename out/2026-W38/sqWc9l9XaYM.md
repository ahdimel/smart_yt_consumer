## The six rules

Her thesis: quit because you have made leaving affordable, not because you hate the job. She says
she left banking after 9 years, 2 months before a six-figure bonus, "taking almost zero financial
risk". The rules follow from that.

**1. Make leaving affordable first (`00:18`).** Quitting in anger swaps work stress for money
stress, and that often sends people back (`01:00`). She gives three ways to cover the gap:

- **Cash (`01:40`).** Hold 6–12 months of *essential* spending: rent, bills, groceries,
  insurance, debt payments. That is more than the usual 3–6 month emergency fund, and she explains
  why (`02:00`). An emergency fund covers something unplanned, and you would start job-hunting
  straight away. Someone who quits by choice gets no redundancy pay and isn't job-hunting, so the
  runway has to be long enough that money doesn't become the daily worry.
- **Other income (`03:08`).** Side business, freelancing, rent or portfolio income that covers
  essentials. Before relying on it, she wants at least **3 months in a row** of it covering them,
  because one good month could be a fluke (`03:50`). The steadier the income, the less cash you
  need.
- **Both (`04:05`).** This is what she did: about 9 months of expenses in cash, with YouTube plus
  portfolio income already covering living costs.

**2. Quiet quit to free up time (`05:20`).** Until August 2022 her banking job came first, and
content was the first thing dropped whenever work got busy. To get a gap she went as far as
interviewing for and accepting another job so she could take time off in between (`06:05`). Her
advice now: do the minimum that protects your reputation, and put the energy you save into the
next thing. The job should fund your next move, not get in its way.

**3. Test the next thing while you still have a salary (`07:04`).** Being different doesn't make
it better. Often people want to leave their situation, not their job. So get a first customer or
make your first £1. Talk to people in the career you want about the parts they hate, and shadow
them if you can. Make videos on your phone before quitting to become a creator. The aim is proof
of concept, not proof it will work.

**4. Work out what staying costs you (`08:45`).** Ask whether the pay is worth what you give up
to earn it. Her figures, pre-tax (`09:05`): over $20,000 a month from the day job against an
expected ~$3,500 a month from the side business. That meant giving up more than $200K a year. She
says it was worth it for her because of fulfilment and because of what the job was costing her
health and relationships. Her counterexample (`10:06`): friends earning $200–300K who hate their
work and rationally stay, because the money pays for a home, a nanny and security for their
children. She calls neither choice wrong.

**5. Save hard in the final months (`11:53`).** Do the opposite of "might as well enjoy the money
while I'm miserable". Cut spending, pay off debt and build cash. Every extra month saved might buy
the business another six months, or let you turn down the wrong offer.

**6. Minimise regret (`12:40`).** If you're still unsure after rules 1–5, picture yourself in 10–20
years, having stayed and having left. Most failure can be recovered from, but some chances only
last so long, because energy, freedom and appetite for risk fade (`14:20`). This is Bezos's
regret-minimisation framework without the credit.

## Assessment

The advice is sound, conventional and clearly organised. There's nothing new here if you've
thought about this for more than an hour. Her "zero risk" claim holds for her: she had 9 months'
cash *and* income already covering expenses, on a $240K+ salary. It is survivor's evidence,
though, and not representative. The 3-month income test and the 6–12 month range are reasonable
rules of thumb, not derived from anything. She's UK-based but quotes "USD equivalent" figures,
which confuses a few viewers.

Cut as fluff: cold-open teaser, the dramatised rage-quit scenario (`00:30`–`01:35`), her video and
workshop plugs (`04:57`–`05:20`), a Shopify sponsor read (`10:58`–`11:53`), a wind-up and some
repetition, a closing restatement and the outro.

## What the comments contest

- **Job searches take longer than people assume (10 likes).** Big-company HR can take 4–6 weeks
  just to respond, so even someone job-hunting from day one needs a bigger buffer than 3–6 months.
- **Rules 2 and 3 assume energy you may not have (4).** A burned-out commenter points out that
  quiet quitting isn't always possible, because workload and managers keep you just tired enough
  to keep going. Testing a side business is also unrealistic if you need 6–8 months to recover.
  Another notes (1) that "the minimum" has crept above a 40-hour week for many workers.
- **Her position is unusual (12, 7).** Viewers point out that walking away from a six-figure bonus
  only makes sense because she was already a high earner.
- **Evidence that it works either way.** One person had 11 months of runway and still failed,
  adding that personal-life stability matters too. Several say they were laid off and that saving
  frugally made it painless.
- **Beyond the video:** skip the exit interview, or keep it positive if you can't (17).

post with 88 likes, and an unsolicited pitch for community-tab management.
