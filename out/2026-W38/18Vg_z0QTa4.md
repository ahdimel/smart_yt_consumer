## The argument

A founder who says he sold his company for nine figures gives eight "laws". The most useful
one is the first. Its main claim is that you should put your money into your own earning
power before you put it into the market.

### 1. The cash waterfall (`0:40`–`4:30`)

Instead of fixed percentages ("save 20%, put 10% in index funds"), he fills four buckets in
order. Each bucket overflows into the next only once it is full:

1. **Safety net (`1:05`).** 3–6 months of expenses. Keep it in a high-yield savings account or
   a government money-market fund, not literal cash; anything you can sell for cash the same
   day is fine. It comes first because a disruption that forces bankruptcy is "the only real
   failure."
2. **Debt that costs more than you can earn (`1:50`).** Apple and Musk carry debt because
   their return beats the interest rate. If yours doesn't, pay the debt off. His threshold is
   anything above 6%.
3. **Your own rate of return (`2:30`).** Index funds return 5–10%, which won't change your
   financial life. Raising your own market value will. His order within this bucket (`2:55`):
   first buy **leverage** (software, employees, contractors) so money does work without your
   time. If you can't afford that, buy skills, ranked as *access* (being in rooms with people
   who have done it), then mentorship or coaching, then vetted and preferably in-person
   education, then books. This bucket has a ceiling, because you can only absorb so much.
4. **The market (`3:55`).** Whatever can no longer raise your own earning power goes into index
   funds and bonds. If you're risk-averse, you can move some of bucket 3 into bucket 4
   earlier (`4:15`).

### 2. Returns come from inefficient markets (`4:30`–`7:20`)

You won't outthink millions of full-time analysts in the S&P 500. Edges exist where
information is scarce. The one example that actually carries evidence is the accountant for his
company's sale: $1,000/hr against $35/hr for a generalist doing the same arithmetic
(`6:20`). His practical advice is to learn one zip code or neighbourhood better than the general
public before buying a house (`6:50`).

Two problems with this section. His definition of an efficient market ("the buying and selling
price are exactly the same", `4:55`) describes a zero bid-ask spread, not market efficiency. His
Mount Fuji example (water at about $0.70 below and $15 at the summit, `5:25`) shows captive
demand plus the cost of hauling water up a mountain, not an information edge, so it doesn't
support his point. The figure is also probably inflated: summit huts are commonly reported to
charge around ¥500–600, roughly $4.

### 3. One income stream first (`7:20`–`8:10`)

"Millionaires have seven income streams" may describe them once they're rich, but it isn't how
they got rich. His claim is to go deep on one stream in an inefficient market, let your
expertise and income compound, and diversify only once that stream can't absorb more
investment. His evidence is only that he knows "hundreds of millionaires and dozens of
billionaires" and none of them did it the other way.

### 4. Save for defence, not to get rich (`8:10`–`9:55`)

Skipping small purchases can't compound into "real wealth." He calls this "almost
mathematically impossible," which is overstated. $1,800 a year at 7% for 40 years is about
$360k, and $6,000 a year is about $1.2M. That's short of what he means by wealth, but far from
nothing. He gives saving a different job: keeping cash ready for rare opportunities that open
for hours or weeks and then close. His example is Buffett holding $397B in cash.

### 5. Asymmetric bets (`9:55`–`11:25`)

He reads Buffett's "rule one: don't lose money" as a two-step method. First ask what the most
you can lose is and how to shrink it, then ask what the most you can gain is. His example is
Branson starting Virgin Atlantic on a one-year lease of a single plane instead of buying one,
which capped the downside at about $2M. He says the method applies to anything you spend on,
from a book to a hire.

### 6–8. Mindset (`11:25`–`14:20`)

- **Read biographies of self-made billionaires (`11:25`).** These give you access to how rich
  people think when you can't get into their rooms. His picks are *Total Recall*
  (Schwarzenegger), *Shoe Dog* (Phil Knight) and *The Hard Thing About Hard Things* (Ben
  Horowitz).
- **Surface your money beliefs (`12:10`).** Write "money is…" without stopping for 60 seconds.
  Then ask of each belief whether it is true, whether it serves you, and what you could
  believe instead.
- **Decide macro before micro (`13:25`).** Pick the neighbourhood, state and point in the cycle
  before choosing a particular house. The same goes for stocks and companies.

The video ends with a plug for his AI video (`14:20`).
