Volkswagen is squeezed by five things at once, and the film's actual argument is that none of
them is individually fatal but the combination is: the cost of the EV transition, the collapse
of China as a profit source, a shrunken European market, Trump's tariffs, and German labour
costs it cannot cut without a fight. The stakes are framed as national — VW is a bellwether for
German and European manufacturing, and nearly a quarter of a million German manufacturing jobs
have disappeared in five years `02:05`.

## The labour fight, and what it actually settled

In September 2024 management proposed closing German plants and cutting wages and jobs `04:07`
— a break with VW's tradition of securing worker approval on major decisions, which put it
against the works council and IG Metall. The settlement: no factory closures, no wage cuts, no
layoffs, but 35,000 jobs going through voluntary redundancy and German production capacity cut
by nearly 750,000 cars a year `05:07`. Critics in the film say this falls short of making VW
globally competitive; one analyst calls it "nowhere near the radical restructuring management
said is needed" `32:28`, and expects the same fight to recur.

The structural reason VW cannot move faster gets the clearest explanation in the film `06:08`.
The Porsche-Piëch family holds the voting majority, but the state of Lower Saxony controls 20%
with effective veto power over key decisions, and the works council holds half the supervisory
board seats under German co-determination law. With most plants in Lower Saxony, the state
usually sides with labour. Investor Ingo Speich's verdict: governance is weak, shareholder
interests are not aligned, every decision is a compromise. The CFO's counter is that VW runs
"on the basis of satisfying all stakeholders" `09:09` — which is presented as the company's
identity rather than defended as a strategy.

The history section explains why that structure exists: VW was founded in 1937 as a Nazi
project, partly with money stolen from German trade unions, and used forced labour during the
war `07:08`. The Allies considered demolishing it, kept it instead, and pushed worker
representation as part of re-democratising German industry `08:09`.

## Why the transition went badly

The EU's 2035 ban on new petrol cars put an expiry date on the technology German carmakers were
best at `11:11`. That forced a double burden — developing EVs while staying competitive in
combustion — and EVs earn less per unit because batteries are expensive. Nearly every European
carmaker issued profit warnings last year `12:11`. Dieselgate, which exposed emissions-cheating
software in more than 10 million cars, made VW's pivot away from diesel more abrupt than the
industry was ready for.

Zwickau is the concrete case: converted in 2019 with Merkel presiding, it became the ID line
`13:11`. The plant manager walks through why an EV is not a retooled combustion car — fully
automated cockpit install, battery assembly near the line, chassis and battery joined at a
"marriage station" `14:20` — and says it needs new people, new suppliers, new processes.

Then demand did not show up. Germany's coalition abruptly cut EV subsidies in 2023, charging
remains a customer objection, the AfD campaigns against EVs, and the early ID.3 was reviewed as
expensive with insufficient range `16:38`. Software was the other failure: VW built Cariad
in-house, hired thousands of coders, shipped late and buggy, sank nearly €12bn, then pulled the
plug and announced a €5bn joint venture with Rivian `17:38` — an admission, per the FT
reporter, that not everything can be done in-house.

## China is the part worth watching

VW entered China in the late 1970s and helped build the market; by the mid-90s more than half
of all cars in China were VW joint-venture vehicles, the Santana a genuine status symbol
`18:38`. The state's 2012 EV strategy under then science minister Wan Gang, plus subsidies at
every level, produced BYD, Nio, Xpeng and a hundred-plus startups — and in 2024 VW lost its
position as China's top-selling brand to BYD `20:40`.

The single most useful minute is a Shanghai buyer explaining what she chose instead `21:41`:
design and colour options, an app that runs the AC before she gets in, an in-car assistant she
uses in place of her phone, apps and video and karaoke, partial self-driving, over-the-air
updates. The competitive axis moved from engineering to software and usability, and VW was
optimising the wrong one. The CFO's response — "I don't think we've been complacent" `25:08` —
sits awkwardly against an analyst's suggestion of hubris in Wolfsburg `19:38`.

Europe offers no relief: roughly 2 million fewer cars sold a year than pre-pandemic, which at
VW's ~25% share is about half a million cars `25:08`. BYD is already landing transporters in
Bremen. The US is the declared growth market `26:10`, but tariffs, global supply chains, and
the residue of Dieselgate make it a hard second attempt.

## The fix on offer

Zwickau loses ID production to Wolfsburg and must find ~20% productivity `29:12`. The product
answer is that ID.7 is better received than ID.3, a more affordable EV lands next year, and an
entry-level ID.1 at €20,000 arrives in 2027 `30:18`. In China, VW moves to in-China-for-China
development, has taken a stake in Xpeng, and says it will be "back in the game" from 2026 —
analysts say two to three years, plus however long trust takes `32:28`. One live option the
film ends on: if plants cannot close, they can be sold, possibly to Chinese EV makers wanting
European production `33:35`. Asked directly, the CFO says they exclude nothing.

## What the comments contest

The comments are less interested in tariffs and governance and more or less unanimous on a
cause the film barely raises: **price and quality**. A new Tiguan at €70k, Golf base prices
roughly doubled in a decade — "so much for the people's car." Several owners and ex-owners date
a quality drop to around 2019 and say a Kia is no longer two or three times worse. Multiple
top-voted comments name capacitive touch controls replacing physical buttons as a
self-inflicted wound, and note that features shipped as standard on cheap Asian cars remain
paid options on VWs.

Three specific challenges to the film's framing are worth flagging:

- **The "didn't see it coming" defence doesn't survive the CFO's own CV.** He says he worked in
  China 2017–2021 and witnessed the transformation; commenters ask how that squares with being
  "thrown off guard."
- **Dieselgate's financial magnitude is underplayed.** Commenters argue the fines left the
  books red before the EV spend began, and that cost-cutting on interiors and generational
  reuse across Škoda/Audi/VW followed from it — a chain the film doesn't draw.
- **The China story is a choice, not a surprise.** VW sold the Santana for nearly 40 years while
  Western customers got new product; commenters read that as having trained and then ceded the
  market.

Unverified in the comments but worth noting as claims, not facts: ~€200bn of group debt, an
ID.3 selling at 20k in China against 40k in Germany, and that the strike wave began after a
large shareholder payout rather than from sales weakness. Two dissenting voices: an ID.4 owner
in Germany who rates it the best car they've driven and blames marketing, and a commenter
noting the new standardised EV platform arriving from 2026 should cut production costs ~25%.
The most cutting line is about the film's own closing minute — "too big to fail" read as
"hubris on steroids."
