Almost none of this advice is specific to having $100K. Apart from the compounding point and
the rising pressure to spend, it is the order of operations usually given to someone with $5K.
The title promises advice for after $100K. What you get is the generic basics.

## The claims

**Lifestyle inflation is the main risk at this stage** (`01:50`–`03:40`). Nischa's framing is
that once your balance looks healthy, spending more feels like a reward you've earned, not a
mistake. Her evidence is her own time in banking: her salary rose from £35K to six figures,
her spending rose with it, and her savings *rate* fell even though she was still saving. The
lesson is to track the share of income you keep, not how much you earn. She calls
status-driven spending the "illusion of affluence" and says researchers use the term, but she
gives no source. Her test before any big upgrade: is this buying the life I want, or the life
that looks good to other people?

**Order of operations** (`04:05`–`05:25`). She says the order matters more than any single step:

1. Pay off anything at double-digit interest first. A 10% market return can't beat a 20%
   credit card.
2. Keep 3–6 months of essential expenses in cash.
3. Only then invest.

**Your career matters more than your portfolio** (`05:25`–`06:07`). Most of your effort should
go into earning more. Investing runs in the background.

**Keep investing simple** (`06:07`–`06:55`). Hitting six figures makes people think they now
need leverage, options and P/E ratios. Unless investing is your job, she says that's one of the
fastest ways to lose the money. Plan around a "freedom number" (the invested amount you'd need
to never worry about money) instead. She doesn't explain how to calculate it here. That's held
back for the workshop.

**Use tax wrappers and the pension** (`07:25`–`10:05`):
- Use ISAs and pensions in the UK, or a Roth IRA in the US. Two people holding the same fund
  with the same returns end up "tens of thousands" apart over 20 years because of tax alone.
  She doesn't show the calculation.
- Take the full employer pension match. She calls it a 100% return, which is only true for a
  1:1 match. Many matches are 50%.
- As wealth grows, check how you pay yourself if you're self-employed, and learn how capital
  gains and dividend tax work. Paying for tax advice is worth it.

**The compounding argument** (`10:11`–`11:50`). She credits Charlie Munger (`01:15`) with the
idea that the first $100K is the hardest because you build it on effort alone. Each $100K
after that comes faster because returns start contributing. This is directionally right, but
she overstates it: at 7% a year, $100K earns about $7K, so for most people contributions still
outweigh returns for years. She also calls investing the one area where more effort gives worse
results, because tinkering hurts returns. The advice is to leave it alone through the "boring
middle."

**Pressure to spend grows with wealth** (`12:14`–`13:50`). Your peer group moves up (private
schools, nicer neighbourhoods, nicer cars), so the pull to keep up is strongest just when you
can finally afford it. Before a big purchase, ask what else the money could have done. This
largely repeats the test from `03:30`.

## What the comments contest

- **Bot flood (92 likes):** "the bots have absolutely decimated the comments." Several of the
  most-liked comments (42, 34, 32 likes) are fake "retired at 54, earning 45K biweekly"
  testimonials, and at least one comment pushes a book.
- **Munger's figure is out of date (3 likes each, several comments):** his $100K was decades
  ago and is roughly $250–450K today. The video never adjusts for this.
- **The order is backwards for this audience (1 like):** someone who has already saved $100K
  should have built an emergency fund and started investing long before. Waiting until $100K
  to start those habits delays the thing that gets people there.
- **Pensions aren't free money (9 likes):** retirement ages keep rising, withdrawals are taxed,
  and politicians keep changing the rules. The video presents pensions without these caveats.
- **The emergency fund matters (10 likes):** a £1K dental emergency without cash means selling
  investments. This supports step 2.
- **Wrappers vary by country (1 like):** Spain has no comparable tax-efficient wrapper, so the
  "every country has one" claim doesn't hold everywhere.
- **Slow start (1 like):** "3 minutes in and you haven't told us a single thing you promised
  to." That's accurate. The first actionable step arrives at `04:05`.
