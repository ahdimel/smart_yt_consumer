Howard Stevenson — the man who built HBS's entrepreneurship curriculum, an early partner in
Baupost, and by his own accounting a three-time failure at dying — spends an hour dismantling
the idea that success is one thing you can win. He opens by naming the research question that
started it: why is it so hard for successful people to have successful children? "Rice paddies
to rice paddies, clogs to clogs." He and Laura Nash interviewed ~150 high achievers to find out,
and the answer turned into a taxonomy rather than a technique.

The framing credentials are personal. He resigned tenure in 1978, immediately after getting it,
because he'd interviewed tenured faculty and found them unhappy — "why would I go down a path
where many of the more quote successful people aren't happy?" (`06:09`). He left Baupost on
becoming a single parent, and still opens Forbes, sees the man who took his seat, and winces
(`07:11`). He is explicit that the wince never went away; the claim is only that he doesn't
regret the choice.

## The four kinds of success

The central move is at `12:12`. Ask people *why* they succeeded and you get Franklin and Covey
boilerplate. Ask them to *tell you about successes in their life* and four separate things fall
out:

- **Achievement** — what you've done against goals others are also striving for. Money, power,
  fame, and you cannot have them all.
- **Significance** — positive impact on the people you care about. Requires choosing *who*;
  even Bill Gates can only give everyone on Earth ten dollars (`15:14`).
- **Happiness** — how you feel about your life, right now. "Happiness is really about me and
  now. You don't make other people happy."
- **Legacy** — impact on the future, and "I'm sorry, Bill Clinton, you don't define your own
  legacy" (`14:13`).

The load-bearing claim is that these four are **uncorrelated** (`13:12`). Hemingway and Monroe
achieved without happiness. His grandfather, an assistant postmaster in small-town Utah, was
significant without achieving. Marx left an enormous legacy while being, by Stevenson's
account, a drunk who was cruel to his family. Getting one does not get you another.

Worse, each domain has what he calls **twins** at `17:14` — a virtuous driver and a vicious one
that produce the same outcome. Achievement runs on recognition, pride, and mastery, or on envy,
greed, and fear. Significance on fairness, generosity, and caring, or on power and
self-aggrandizement. Happiness on contentment and fulfillment, or on laziness and gluttony.
Legacy on altruism and generativity, or on fear of death and the need for control — his example
is a man who wrote a thousand-year trust, to which Stevenson notes that William the Conqueror
still has fifty-odd years left to run. And the virtuous twin is often the *less* effective one:
contentment doesn't help you achieve, and "certain neuroses help you to achieve."

## Why the two obvious strategies fail

**Find your passion** (`19:17`): no single activity delivers all four. Different constituencies,
different judgments, different skills. "If you try and be CEO at home, the chief operating
officer generally has something to say about it." Focus on one and you get collateral damage on
the others. He's equally blunt about passion as career advice — the parents subsidizing an actor
until the actor turns fifty and the subsidy dies with them.

**Sequential success** (`20:18`) — achieve first, find significance later, then happiness, then
leave a legacy — is the genre sold to YPO members under titles like *Halftime*, and it fails on
one question: when is enough to move on? There is no largest integer; you can always want one
more. Emotional needs don't wait their turn. Neither do children: "my youngest is 38 and my
oldest is 53, and they're as needy now as they were at 17 — there's just another zero in all the
needs." And you will miss things. "Can you wait to be happy?"

What the satisfied interviewees did instead was a **spiral** (`23:20`): small versions of all
four, early, told as stories from high school and first jobs, growing outward. The legacies they
valued most were the ones seeded youngest, before they were trying.

## Juggling, not balance

"I hate the word balance. I always think of it as a seal with something spinning on his nose."
The operative metaphor is juggling (`27:28`), and he takes it literally:

- Keep your eye on **all** the balls; watch one and you drop the rest.
- Every time you touch a ball you must give it **energy and direction** and then let it go.
  "Nobody applauds you as a juggler if you hold all the balls in your hand."
- The most important ball is not the one at the top or the one in your hand. It is **the falling
  ball** (`28:28`).
- Some balls are rubber and some shatter. Careers bounce. Family doesn't.

He also puts a floor under happiness: if you don't know by your thirties what makes you happy,
stop and work it out (`29:28`). His own answer is unglamorous — lunch with friends, talking to
each of his kids two or three times a week — and the point is that he *schedules* it rather than
deferring it.

## Enough

The third finding, and the one he flags as the biggest surprise, is at `34:33`. "Enough" is an
odd English word because it has both a lower bound (*have I done enough?*) and an upper bound
(*that's enough!*), and almost nobody defines the upper one. Without it you are committed to
more, and more crowds out everything else.

He wants it defined on two axes (`35:35`): across the four domains, and across time — enough for
today, this week, this year, a lifetime. His own devices are concrete. He keeps money given away
on his personal balance sheet, because dying richest in the cemetery isn't the goal but he is
"a measurement type of guy." He builds a day's list he can actually finish, and starts with the
quick items so the day closes as a win.

The payoff is counterintuitive and is really the thesis of the talk: **setting limits increases
the dimensionality of success** (`40:36`). A defined "enough" both motivates you toward it and
releases you once you're there — which is precisely what lets you throw the ball and catch the
next one.

Frank Batten of Landmark Communications is the worked example (`43:39`): "My purpose is serving
my customers at a profit. Profit is a constraint, not my goal. If I make too much money, it says
I'm probably not reinvesting enough in my people, my community, and my product" (`44:41`).
Batten would have paid $250,000 to get *off* the Forbes list, and gave HBS $35 million in six
weeks when Stevenson skipped the pitch and asked to be number five on his list.

## The closing rules

- **Start at the end** (`45:42`). Nobody publishes your balance sheet in your obituary.
- **You will not get an A+ in everything.** Decide what to let go.
- **"Everybody's outside looks better than my inside"** (`46:43`) — the line the comments seized
  on, delivered a decade before it became the standard diagnosis of social media.
- **Inflection points are gifts** (`47:43`), including the ones that arrive as disasters. His
  wife leaving and his resignation of tenure are both on the list. Most people pass them without
  noticing they've been handed a free change of direction.
- **Is the juice worth the squeeze?** If you want orange juice, don't squeeze grapefruit. HBS
  graduates are competitive enough to run any race whose gun goes off, including being first to
  the dump on Saturday.
- **Don't cheat at solitaire** (`12:12`). Regret, he argues, comes from kidding yourself, not
  from bad outcomes.
- **Be explicit about your bets** (`48:43`). Three states: won, lost, still unknown. People will
  not say "lost" — they say the world is changing and they'll make it up next time.
- **The culture question** (`49:43`). Some places are toxic; some are fine for others and wrong
  for you.
- **Live life forward.** The Irish minister of reconciliation explaining a 17th-century land
  theft as an ongoing grievance, the Slovenians litigating 1400 or 400 depending on the faction
  (`50:44`). You can learn from the past; you cannot change it.
- **Not mentors — a personal board of directors** (`51:45`). He doesn't want anyone's advice on
  *all* of his life. He has the data to back the reframe: when HBS asked faculty to name mentors
  across teaching, research, and course development, nobody got more than 17 mentions, almost
  nobody was named in all three categories, and some people who considered themselves mentors
  were named by no one — "perfectly willing to give advice, it just wasn't listened to"
  (`52:46`). Six or seven is the most you can maintain.
- **Plan for the ripple, not the splash** (`54:46`). After 43 years of teaching, the satisfying
  part is the people who turn up and say you changed something you don't remember saying.

The test he leaves is four questions: who are you and what are your values; which satisfactions
are you on the way to missing; who is important to you and are you helping them succeed; what's
your time frame. Time allowed for the test: the rest of your life.

## What the comments contest

The comments don't really contest the argument — 2.9M views' worth of agreement is the dominant
note, with "marry a happy person" and "success is getting what you want, happiness is wanting
what you get" quoted back most often. Three things are worth flagging:

**The date of the death doesn't add up.** A high-voted comment quotes Wikipedia saying the
unattended cardiac arrest happened "in 2014, on January 5." Stevenson describes it in the past
tense in a talk the channel labels 2013, and dates it to January 3rd. One of those is wrong; the
transcript can't tell you which, but the video's own metadata rules out the 2014 version.

**The Trump material reads differently now than it did.** Five separate comments are just
delight at the 2013-vintage jabs ("blud hates DTrump from way back"). This is the clearest case
in the talk of an audience taking a throwaway line as the headline — Stevenson uses Trump purely
as a stock example of envy-driven achievement, and the joke has outlived the point it was
attached to.

**One dissent worth keeping.** "Man this is really good advice and depression at the same time.
It makes everything in life seem so shallow — it's like knowing how the movie ends before
watching it." That's a fair reaction to a framework that tells you to pre-define enough in four
domains, and the talk doesn't answer it. The closest it comes is the ice cream story (`12:12`):
an interview stopped mid-sentence because the ice cream had arrived and would melt.
