## The claim

One load-bearing assertion: Treasury and the Fed *say* they are curbing spending and inflation,
while their actual flows point to roughly $1.3T of new net liquidity over the coming year — and the
resulting inflation is not a side effect but the mechanism for shrinking the real value of a $40T
debt pile. Vance's "very discreet plan" (`02:00`) is the hook; the Cantillon effect is the body.

## The ledger

Net liquidity = Fed balance sheet − reverse repo − Treasury General Account. The accounting runs
`03:30`–`11:10` and is the part worth watching, because the charts do work prose can't.

- **TGA** (`04:30`): ~$1T, highest since COVID. Bessent tripled Treasury buyback operations from
  $2B to $6B (`04:50`), ~$100B already drawn in a month; reversion to the $400B long-run average
  would release ~$500B (`05:15`). Explicitly not QE — existing balances, not new money (`05:50`).
- **Fed balance sheet** (`06:10`): peaked ~$9T in June 2022, ran off $2.4T, now expanding again via
  ~$250B of T-bill purchases. The Fed's own projection is $7.5T within a year, ≈$1T of new
  reserves (`08:00`). Reserve-creation mechanics at `07:00` if you need them.
- **Reverse repo** (`08:20`): drained, and refills only if the Fed's parking rate beats bills. At
  3.75% against ~4% bills it doesn't (`10:00`).

Net liquidity $5.8T → $7.1T, i.e. back to 2021 (`11:00`).

The single sharpest thirty seconds is `10:00`: the rate hike everyone is reacting to is
liquidity-*neutral*, because the arbitrage that sucked $2.5T into reverse repo in 2022 isn't
available at today's spread. That is a specific, checkable observation.

## Channel and incidence

`14:10`: net liquidity shifted forward ~12 months tracks core inflation. `15:30`: the five-stage
Cantillon staircase — banks, then corporates and contractors, then asset prices, then consumer
prices, then wages. The tax is collected at stages 4 and 5, where inflation has already eroded the
dollar before wages catch it; the $100-at-10% worked example is at `17:15`. `19:18`: top-1% wealth
has tracked M2 growth, bottom-50% wealth grew about a fifth as fast. The kicker at `20:19` is that
in previous hiking episodes net worth fell for *both* groups even as liquidity rose.

## Where it's weak

**The buyback-as-TGA-drawdown step.** Treasury buybacks are normally funded inside the regular
financing schedule via offsetting bill issuance, not by spending down the checking account. If
that's right, the $500B injection is largely a reclassification, and it is load-bearing for the
whole net-liquidity path. The video never raises it.

**The hiking premise is asserted, never shown.** "The Fed is going to be hiking," "violently"
(`12:20`, `20:19`) is stated as fact and used to sell the offer, while the same video has the Fed
buying bills and targeting $7.5T. Those can coexist; the video doesn't reconcile them.

**Inflating away the debt ignores duration.** With a short average maturity and interest already a
top budget line, higher inflation raises refinancing cost faster than it erodes principal.

**The 1%-vs-bottom-50% chart is rhetoric, not measurement.** No group's wealth has reason to grow
1:1 with M2, and the bottom half holds almost no assets by construction. The distributional point is
probably correct; this isn't evidence for it. Same for the 12-month lead, eyeballed off one chart
containing essentially one episode.

## What the comments contest

The best objection (7 likes): the bond market is in control, not the Fed — you can't inflate away
debt when debt service is your largest line item and rising faster than inflation, and bond buyers
won't keep eating the spread. The video's thesis has no answer to this.

Unanswered question (3): how do liquidity expansion and rate hikes happen simultaneously — the
reconciliation the video skips.

On core inflation (4 + 3 likes, `14:28`): food has risen continuously for years, and food and energy
are coupled to the same oil and fertilizer inputs being stripped out. Excluding them excludes what
hits low-income households hardest — which undercuts the video's own incidence argument.

A competing read of the same actions (2): Bessent's operation as "quantitative bond issuance,"
swapping governments for illiquid corporates, implying a higher deficit; the Fed hiking to forestall
curve steepening. Another (1) expects the hikes reversed, since banks are underwater on bond
portfolios and some Fed members are already talking them down.

One genuinely novel addition (1 like): large tech firms spend 1–5% of cash flow on labor versus
roughly a third historically, so liquidity truncates at stage 3 and may never reach stage-5 wages.
Unsourced, and it would make the stealth tax worse rather than milder.

The rest splits between generational disputes over who the transfer reaches (80, 11, 9) and
political venting. No astroturf beyond the creator's own offer.
