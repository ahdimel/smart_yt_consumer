## The claim the video exists to make

`13:30`, flagged in the video as the one thing that should survive it: both camps in this argument
are sold the diagnosis that flatters them, because a flattering diagnosis is the best product in an
attention economy. The algorithm is indifferent to which sex gets blamed and sensitive only to
whether the viewer leaves feeling innocent, and innocent viewers come back. "He was intimidated by
you" is that product for women; "men wanted a servant, men are angry they lost their slaves" is the
identical product with a different customer, sourced to the mirror-image channels asking why women
won't be wives, whose top comments run "Why don't they marry each other and just leave us alone?"
(5,300 likes) and "If marriage benefited women, they would have taken that from us already"
(`12:06`–`13:30`). The structural tell is grammatical: in both versions every verb belongs to the
other side, so nothing in the speaker requires examining `01:20`. A diagnosis that flatters is a
diagnosis nobody tries to cure.

## The experimental hinge

Two studies carry the rest (`02:40`–`04:20`). The Columbia speed-dating experiments (QJE 2006,
revealed rather than stated preferences) found men did say yes to intelligent women — the cartoon
that men want them dumb and decorative fails — but that the valuation had a ceiling: in the paper's
own words men did not value intelligence or ambition exceeding their own, and were significantly less
likely to select a woman who read as more ambitious than they were. Park, Young & Eastwick (PSPB
2015, six studies) found a smart woman described on paper grew *more* desirable the smarter she got,
while the same woman outperforming a man in the room, in real time, grew less so.

The inference `04:20`: if men feared female success as such, the résumé alone would repel at a
distance. It doesn't — so what shifts in the room is not her success but the shape of the
interaction, in which she has been placed as his opponent. That reframing is the point of the video,
because it changes what is fixable: a salary can't change, an interaction can `05:00`.

## The five interactions

All five come from reading comment sections under this channel's and similar videos — labelled
testimony rather than data `05:00`.

1. **Contempt** `05:45`. Gottman's filmed couples: the strongest single divorce predictor was not
conflict, money or sex but contempt, which leaks before anyone speaks — in how she talks about her
ex, in "all men are trash" worn as a personality, in treating a first date like an applicant
suspected of padding his résumé. If this is best behaviour, the marriage is worse; he exits without
feedback, and the silence gets read back to her as intimidation.

2. **The scoreboard** `06:50`. Workplace logic imported into the living room — who earns more, whose
time is worth more, who wins the argument. What it exhausts is not his ego but the partnership;
teammate and rival are different positions. Top male comment under the source short, 927 likes: "We
want a partner, not a competitor."

3. **The empty ledger** `07:40`. A woman texted every man she knew for days to help move a granite
slab fifteen feet, nobody came, and she concluded on camera that they don't make men like they used
to. Gouldner's 1960 norm of reciprocity, present in every culture studied, is the rule she broke:
people keep helping people who give something back. Men didn't become less helpful; they stopped
being vending machines.

4. **The wrong currency** `08:50`. Buss 1989 (37 cultures, 10,000+ people): long-term mate criteria
differ from short-term ones, and the difference replicates on every continent. Against that,
OkCupid's 2009 user data — women rated 80% of men below average in looks, then messaged men across
the whole curve anyway. A presence optimised for maximum attention is broadcasting in the currency
that pays out in attention, not investment.

5. **The restraint tax** `10:10`, the section the video is built to reach. Archer's 2000
meta-analysis: women were slightly more likely to commit an act of physical aggression against a
partner, while men's aggression did far more damage (62% of the injured were women). The cost of that
asymmetry inside a relationship is that he must absorb the slap, the shove, the public humiliation
with total restraint — which the video agrees he should, since his retaliation breaks bones — while
nobody applies the matching standard to provocation: "not hitting him is the floor, not the ceiling."
Restraint supplied by one side and provocation by the other is a job with no pay and no exit
interview.

## The numbers

Pew, `00:00` and `14:07`: single men actively looking for a relationship fell from 61% (2019) to 50%
(2022) while single women barely moved, and 57% of single adults aren't looking at all — an 11-point
three-year drop concentrated in one sex. But 63% of never-married adults under 50 still want to marry
someday, with no meaningful gender gap. Desire held; participation fell. The video reads that as
arithmetic rather than fear: expected value of raising a hand went negative.

## Where it's weaker than its confidence

The hinge is oversold. The Columbia result is a perception effect inside a four-minute date — nobody
competes with anybody there — so it supports plain status comparison, i.e. the intimidation story
being dismantled, just as well as it supports the interaction reading. Only the Park/Eastwick
manipulation is genuinely head-to-head, and those authors read their own finding as self-threat and
psychological distance *in the man*, which is nearer to "intimidated" than the video concedes.
Relabelling it "the interaction" is what makes it actionable for a female viewer; that's a rhetorical
gain, not an empirical one. The five interactions come from a self-selected audience already sorted
for this view — coordination isn't the threat to validity, selection is, and `05:30` answers the
wrong objection. Gottman's >90% accuracy is a retrospectively fitted number. The OkCupid figure is a
company blog post. The 2019→2022 collapse spans the pandemic, a confound never raised. And the
provocation asymmetry in #5 is asserted without any frequency evidence; Archer measures damage, not
who starts it.

What holds up: the market-for-explanations mechanism, which needs none of the studies, and the point
that all five named items are conduct rather than status, so the prescription is never "be less"
`16:30`. It also turns the same blade on its own audience `16:58`, which is rare here.

## What the comments contest

Near-total male agreement and almost no pushback — itself a datum about the "testimony" method.

The sharpest contest is causal. The video's five items are all interpersonal conduct; commenters keep
naming institutional exposure instead. "They don't objectively fear powerful women.. they fear
divorce" (78). A longer one (166) describes proving to a family court that he could support his child
while being ordered to fund the mother who couldn't; another (52) names HR accusations that need no
evidence. On that account how she shows up in the room isn't the variable at all. Divorce and custody
law go unmentioned in 18 minutes.

Second, several dispute that conduct is fixable because the threshold moves: "the goalpost ALWAYS
moves" (378), "being a good man meant you had to be a perfect man" (351), "we stopped qualifying"
(157).

Third, a real refinement of #3 (183): relationships are inherently transactional, so the goal isn't
"no ledger" but enough mutual giving that nobody needs one. And (709) supplies the missing half of
the contempt mechanism — men reject covertly because feedback got them roasted, so the silent exit is
learned rather than innate.

Exactly one comment (152, "the world does not owe you a good man") is aimed at the audience the video
was addressing. Minor pollution: one plug for a dating-advice personality (92), and one long "my
marriage ended a week ago" post (216) of the shape that reliably attracts spiritual-healer scam
replies.
