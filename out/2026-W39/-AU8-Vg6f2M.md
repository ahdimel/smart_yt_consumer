## The answer to the title question

Governments borrow from three places: their own citizens, other arms of their own government
(Social Security trust funds and equivalents), and foreign governments `01:20`. For the US the
first two are ~65% of the total; foreign governments hold ~$9.3T `01:45`. The UK and France look
the same `05:20`. So the title mostly dissolves — the lender is the domestic population.

The non-obvious part is how a population carrying $18T of its own debt has $40T to lend `05:40`.
The three-person diagram at `06:04` is the video's cleanest moment: A borrows 100K from B, later
lends 50K to C, C lends 25K to B. Gross debt is 175K; no outside money entered. Global debt is
that, with 8 billion participants. Gross stock says nothing about net position.

The reason none of it gets repaid is a single institutional innovation: 18th-century Britain
began servicing bonds by issuing new bonds rather than from taxes `04:04`. Every state copied it.
The video calls this "kind of genius" and never engages the obvious objection.

## Why rates are rising without anyone refusing to lend

The load-bearing claim is at `10:30`: lenders don't need to stop, they only need to reprice risk.
Treasury default risk was treated as ~zero, so rates were low; sustainability doubts add a
premium, and on a debt stock this size a couple of points is enormous. Because the network is
circular, one broken link propagates, which is why yields rise everywhere at once `10:55`.

A second, independent driver is inflation `11:20`. Fisher: nominal rate = real rate + expected
inflation. G7 inflation peaked at 6–10% post-2020 and still sits at 3–4% against a 2% target, so
lenders permanently demand more. Consequence already visible: France burned five prime ministers
in two years and forced a budget through without a parliamentary vote `13:07`.

What an actual stop looks like: the UK mini-budget, September 2022 `13:40`. ~£45bn of unfunded
tax cuts, markets judged them unpayable, yields spiraled, and it stopped only when Truss resigned.
The useful qualifier follows at `14:40` — that was fixable because it was *one reversible policy*
plus a credibility swap. Decades of structural deficits offer no such switch.

The US can't be forced into nominal default because its debt is in a currency it issues `15:09`,
but printing doesn't erase the debt, it reassigns it: inflation taxes holders of cash, savings and
the bonds themselves, spread over time instead of delivered at once `15:40`. And it self-feeds —
printing raises inflation expectations, which raise rates, which raise the servicing cost `16:09`.

## The best segment, and it's last

`16:40`–`17:55` is the only part worth watching in full. Nobody fixes this because the payoffs are
asymmetric in time: fixing means cutting spending and raising taxes now — concentrated, immediate,
career-ending — while ignoring it costs a few basis points a year, diffuse across millions of
people and unattributable to any officeholder. Letting debt grow is individually rational for
every decision-maker in the chain. This is the video's actual thesis and it's buried at the end.

## Where it's wrong

The $300–350T figure is *all* global debt — household, corporate and government. At `04:40` the
video says "governments owe $300 trillion to one another," which is off by roughly 3x; global
public debt is ~$100T. The scale visualization built on it `04:45` inherits the error.

"Debt interest is one of, if not the single biggest line item in government budgets" `08:50` is
overstated: US net interest is large and near the defense budget, but well below Social Security
or health spending.

## What the comments contest

- **Map error, `02:12`** (19 + 9 + several more): the large red blob is Japan, not China. Multiple
  independent catches; the video's own cartogram misidentifies its central example.
- **Ponzi framing, `04:10`** (40, 24, and echoed widely): commenters name the roll-debt-with-debt
  mechanism as a Ponzi scheme, legal only for sovereigns. The video presents it approvingly and
  never addresses the charge.
- **Printing mechanics** (8): the Treasury can't "print" — it issues bonds the Fed may purchase.
  The correction is mechanically right; the appended claim that the Fed is "privately owned" is the
  standard misreading of its member-bank structure.
- **Title not answered** (3): "Who's lending the money... still don't know." One low-vote comment
  supplies what the video omits — US net international investment position ≈ -$27.5T, China plus
  Hong Kong ≈ +$6.3T. Net position is the question; the video only ever discusses gross.
- **Banks create deposits** (0): the three-person diagram models lending as pure intermediation of
  existing savings. Commercial bank lending creates deposits, which weakens the diagram's claim
  that "the money simply came from each other."
- **Sectoral balances** (0): if corporate and household sectors both run surpluses, some sector must
  run the deficit. A real macro objection to treating government debt as a standalone pathology.
- **Nordics** (1, 2, 1): Norway and the Nordics contradict "pretty much every Western country."

The section is partly polluted — gold/silver buying plugs and two overtly antisemitic "who really
owns the debt" comments with nonzero likes.
