## The trick in the title

`09:30`–`11:50`. Three steps, for a conversation where you need to be heard and are not being
heard. **Pause three seconds** after the other person stops. **Make exactly two points**, one of
them carried by a gesture (point at it, hold a hand out) so a visual channel runs alongside the
verbal one. **Close with one question** that asks them to act on or repeat one of the two points.

The mechanism she gives for each step — anterior cingulate cortex activating during the pause,
"dual-track working memory" capping you at two items, a question interrupting the default mode
network — is decoration. The tactic stands without it, and the crowd has a real objection to step
one (below). This is the only segment in the video that is a procedure rather than a fact plus a
suggestion, and it is not original to her; see the comments.

## The rest, in order of how much they'll change your day

**Say less per turn.** MIT chunking research is invoked for the claim that listener attention
collapses after ~12 seconds of uninterrupted speech `12:10`, so speak in 5–10 second bursts and
stop. The genuinely useful corollary `13:50`: in an argument, keeping your own replies under ten
seconds eventually shortens theirs. (The claim that TikTok built its algorithm on this research is
invented — she offers nothing for it.)

**Simple words read as high IQ, not low.** `03:30` Munich study: listeners rate plain language as
smarter and more trustworthy, and technical language as concealment. Her worked example `08:10` is
the founder email — "please find attached the subject matter of this email which is in regards to"
versus "quick follow-up on the one thing from last week."

**Concrete beats abstract.** `17:20` Cornell: perceptual phrasing is trusted more than conceptual.
"We must improve operational efficiency" → "fewer steps, smoother handoffs." Related and better
than the cliché it corrects `17:45`: nobody is specifically a visual learner, everyone retains more
when they can see the thing, so draw it.

**Lead with the pattern break.** `02:30` The orienting response: novelty commandeers processing, so
your first sentence does more work than the rest of the conversation. Openers she offers: "Can I
ask you something I've never asked before?" "Can I test an idea on you?"

**Ask instead of assert.** `04:30` Carnegie Mellon: open questions release dopamine and pull people
toward you. The usable artifact is one line from a stalled deal `05:35` — "What would make this
sale feel like a relief instead of a risk?" — aimed at a seller who was visibly panicking about his
own financials.

**Rhythm is read as truth.** `07:30` Princeton processing-fluency work: statements that rhyme or
scan get judged more accurate, because fluent processing feels like correctness. Hence one-liners
("you repeat what you don't repair"), and hence the strategic payoff `09:00` — a line short enough
to repeat gets repeated, and people who repeat your words follow you.

**Your voice sets their pulse.** `06:25` UCL: listeners entrain heart rate to a speaker's rhythm.
Rushed speech transmits stress; steady speech transmits safety. Same premise as the opening claim
`00:30` that mirror neurons fire in ~200ms, so the emotional signal lands before the content does —
which is why calm is a tool and not a temperament.

**Equal airtime substitutes for agreement.** `22:40` Harvard: trust tracks roughly balanced
speaking time, not shared conclusions. You can disagree completely and still be trusted if you give
the floor. The reclaiming line is good and transferable `24:00`: "I just gave you the full two
minutes — I think it's reasonable for you to give it back. Would that be unreasonable?"

**Then close with a recommendation, not a question.** `24:20` People act on a recommendation 60%
more often than an open-ended ask, so end with "here's what we do next," not "let me know what you
think."

**Four small ones.** Replace "I think" with "I've observed" — Columbia, observations read 40% more
credible than opinions `19:12`. Add a time anchor to any ask; deadlines move people `19:45`. Pull a
drifting listener back by using their name plus one specific detail they said `20:15`. Gesture
*before* you speak — UC Berkeley, hands precede speech in the brain — and never hide your hands
behind you, which reads as concealment `14:10`. Roll shoulders back and open the rib cage `21:15`;
she claims cortisol down, testosterone up, and explicitly distances this from power posing.

## Where the confidence outruns the evidence

Every tactic arrives welded to a named institution, and the welds are bad. The Stanford story claim
is stated as "22 times more than facts" and then, four seconds later, as "22% more" `16:10` — two
numbers an order of magnitude apart, in the same breath, for the same study. The mirror-neuron
opener `00:30` states as established fact a reading of Parma's monkey data that the field has spent
twenty years walking back. The postural cortisol/testosterone claim `21:15` is power posing with a
new label, and power posing failed replication; she preemptively denies the resemblance rather than
addressing it.

Two claims also contradict each other and she never notices: end with a question (step three of
3-2-1, `11:00`) versus never end with a question, end with a recommendation (`24:20`).

Take this as a well-curated list of things good communicators actually do — most of it is sound and
some of it is genuinely sharp — and treat the citations as ornament. It is the stronger reading of
the video, not the weaker one: the advice does not need the studies.

## What the comments contest

**The three-second pause fails in exactly the situation it's sold for**, and four separate
commenters say so independently (93, 26, 22, 16 likes — ~157 combined): pause and the other person
reads it as their turn and plows through you. One adds the meeting case — stay quiet and they
decide without you and "treat me like a wall." The video's own PE anecdote assumes you have enough
standing that silence reads as authority; the crowd is pointing out the tactic inverts when you
don't. Related, 696 likes: "when I say something nobody reacts, but if another person repeats
exactly what I said, suddenly everyone finds the idea interesting" — the whole framework assumes
the room is willing to hear you.

**The 3-2-1 method is credited to Jefferson Fischer** (15 likes), which fills the gap she leaves at
`09:30` when she says she can't remember where she stole it from.
