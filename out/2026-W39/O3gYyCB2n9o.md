## The argument

The load-bearing claim is not that AI will cause layoffs. It is that the layoffs are
individually rational and collectively ruinous, so a CEO who sees the cliff coming still drives
at it (`03:40`). Everything else is scaffolding for that.

The mechanism is a single comparison. With one monopoly firm, its workers are its customers, so
the demand it destroys by automating lands back on its own books; it internalises the cost and
automates only partway (`01:15`). Split that firm into a hundred competitors and the accounting
inverts: any one firm's workers spend most of their wages at the other ninety-nine, so the
demand that firm destroys is almost entirely someone else's problem. The private cost of
replacement falls to roughly zero while the cost saving stays whole (`02:05`). Every firm runs
the same sum, demand collapses, and all of them end up worse off than before (`03:05`).

Because automating is the best response whether or not rivals automate, it is a dominating
strategy — Tsoukalas names the prisoner's dilemma outright (`04:05`). That also kills the
voluntary fix. If Anthropic, OpenAI, Microsoft and Google agreed in a room to slow down, each
would face its largest defection payoff the moment the others were committed, and fiduciary duty
points the same way (`09:15`). His flat conclusion: "waiting for the firms to figure it out for
themselves is the worst possible thing we can do" (`10:05`).

So the correction has to be external. They tested about six proposals circulating among
economists, including universal basic income and giving workers equity, and report that only one
survives: an instrument aimed directly at the replacement incentive (`06:20`). Concretely, tax
the full replacement of a worker while leaving augmentation untaxed — a firm can buy its people
AI, but not buy its way out of them. The nearest precedent he offers is carbon pricing, which
prices an externality rather than forbidding it (`07:10`); since "tax" is politically toxic he
floats the mirror image, subsidies for firms that retain workers. If nothing is done, his
prediction is wealth inequality beyond anything on record and the instability that follows, with
2008 as the reference point (`08:20`) — offered explicitly as a personal guess on a question he
says economists split evenly on, not as a model output.

### Where it is thinner than it sounds

The model assumes AI becomes capable of mass replacement; that premise is imported wholesale
from CEO press statements and never defended. The genuinely interesting result — *why* UBI and
worker equity fail inside the model while a replacement tax works — is asserted in one clause
and never shown, and it is the one thing a reader could not have derived unaided. And a
replacement tax in one jurisdiction has an obvious escape hatch that the interview never raises.

## What the comments contest

The premise the video rests on is the one the comments reject hardest. "'CEOs are rational' is a
ridiculous premise" leads at 2,300 likes; a reply at 115 sharpens it into something the model
should answer — they *are* rational, but over a personal payoff on a quarterly horizon, not the
firm's ten-year one (echoed at 444 and 80 likes). That is a different and worse game than the
one the paper solves.

The most substantive correction is Dodge v. Ford Motor Co. (1919), at 369 likes: self-restraint
isn't merely unlikely, it is legally exposed. Ford held back profits to raise wages and cut
prices, the Dodge brothers sued, and the court held that the duty runs to shareholder profit.
On that reading the trap is written into corporate law, which strengthens the paper's case for
an external instrument rather than weakening it.

Other points the video never addresses: 223 likes argue the model assumes the players still want
a functioning consumer economy, and that the destination is techno-feudalism rather than
collapse. 35 likes note the top 10% of US earners already account for 50% of US consumption —
that does not avoid the trap, but it means firms can go far deeper into it before demand visibly
breaks, which a symmetric-worker model will miss. Several comments (23, 18, 14 likes) observe
that three years into the LLM boom the evidence for actual mass replacement is still absent.
62 likes raise a channel the remedy doesn't fund: workers are also the tax base, so government
revenue collapses alongside demand. 12 likes offer Japan's dismissal-protection law as an
existing, already-tested version of the instrument. And from 1979, British Leyland's Derek
Robinson, shown the new robot production line: "Yes, and they won't buy your cars either"
(7 likes) — the whole paper in nine words.
