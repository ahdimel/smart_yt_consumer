The argument

The load-bearing claim is not that AI will cause layoffs. It is that the layoffs are individually rational and collectively ruinous, so a CEO who sees the cliff coming still drives at it (03:40). Everything else is scaffolding for that.

The mechanism is a single comparison. With one monopoly firm, its workers are its customers, so the demand it destroys by automating lands back on its own books; it internalises the cost and automates only partway (01:15). Split that firm into a hundred competitors and the accounting inverts: any one firm's workers spend most of their wages at the other ninety-nine, so the demand that firm destroys is almost entirely someone else's problem. The private cost of replacement falls to roughly zero while the cost saving stays whole (02:05). Every firm runs the same sum, demand collapses, and all of them end up worse off than before (03:05).

Because automating is the best response whether or not rivals automate, it is a dominating strategy — Tsoukalas names the prisoner's dilemma outright (04:05). That also kills the voluntary fix. If Anthropic, OpenAI, Microsoft and Google agreed in a room to slow down, each would face its largest defection payoff the moment the others were committed, and fiduciary duty points the same way (09:15). His flat conclusion: "waiting for the firms to figure it out for themselves is the worst possible thing we can do" (10:05).

So the correction has to be external. They tested about six proposals circulating among economists, including universal basic income and giving workers equity, and report that only one survives: an instrument aimed directly at the replacement incentive (06:20). Concretely, tax the full replacement of a worker while leaving augmentation untaxed — a firm can buy its people AI, but not buy its way out of them. The nearest precedent he offers is carbon pricing, which prices an externality rather than forbidding it (07:10); since "tax" is politically toxic he floats the mirror image, subsidies for firms that retain workers. If nothing is done, his prediction is wealth inequality beyond anything on record and the instability that follows, with 2008 as the reference point (08:20) — offered explicitly as a personal guess on a question he says economists split evenly on, not as a model output.

Where it is thinner than it sounds

The model assumes AI becomes capable of mass replacement; that premise is imported wholesale from CEO press statements and never defended. The genuinely interesting result — why UBI and worker equity fail inside the model while a replacement tax works — is asserted in one clause and never shown, and it is the one thing a reader could not have derived unaided. And a replacement tax in one jurisdiction has an obvious escape hatch that the interview never raises.

What the comments contest

The premise the video rests on is the one the comments reject hardest. "'CEOs are rational' is a ridiculous premise" leads at 2,300 likes; a reply at 115 sharpens it into something the model should answer — they are rational, but over a personal payoff on a quarterly horizon, not the firm's ten-year one (echoed at 444 and 80 likes). That is a different and worse game than the one the paper solves.

The most substantive correction is Dodge v. Ford Motor Co. (1919), at 369 likes: self-restraint isn't merely unlikely, it is legally exposed. Ford held back profits to raise wages and cut prices, the Dodge brothers sued, and the court held that the duty runs to shareholder profit. On that reading the trap is written into corporate law, which strengthens the paper's case for an external instrument rather than weakening it.

Other points the video never addresses: 223 likes argue the model assumes the players still want a functioning consumer economy, and that the destination is techno-feudalism rather than collapse. 35 likes note the top 10% of US earners already account for 50% of US consumption — that does not avoid the trap, but it means firms can go far deeper into it before demand visibly breaks, which a symmetric-worker model will miss. Several comments (23, 18, 14 likes) observe that three years into the LLM boom the evidence for actual mass replacement is still absent. 62 likes raise a channel the remedy doesn't fund: workers are also the tax base, so government revenue collapses alongside demand. 12 likes offer Japan's dismissal-protection law as an existing, already-tested version of the instrument. And from 1979, British Leyland's Derek Robinson, shown the new robot production line: "Yes, and they won't buy your cars either" (7 likes) — the whole paper in nine words.

The argument

Congress has fixed the US carrier force at 11 hulls by law since 2007, yet the Navy has recently struggled to have more than three on operation at once (00:20, 00:33). The video's real claim is that this gap has two independent causes that feed each other: ships take too long to build and refit, and the ones that work are deployed too long — which makes them take even longer to refit.

Supply side (00:55–02:20). USS Gerald R. Ford entered service four years late and $3bn over budget; John F. Kennedy is nearly four years behind and only now in sea trials. Because the new hulls are late, the old ones can't leave: Nimitz is past 50 years and should have been retired, Eisenhower hits 50 next year, and neither is fully operational. John C. Stennis is five-plus years into its mid-life overhaul and reactor refuelling, 14 months behind schedule. The stated causes — technical glitches plus shortages of specific skilled trades — are not the kind of thing that can be fixed inside a budget cycle (02:04).

A third Ford-class redesign would make it worse: the Navy says it is considering one because Trump objects to the electromagnetic catapults and wants steam back (02:24). The narrator says "for some reason," which is where the video is weakest — it declines to state the actual case, that EMALS reliability problems are themselves among the reasons Ford was late. The aside about possibly naming the next carrier Donald Trump is colour, not evidence.

Demand side (03:25–05:10). In 2003 the Navy put six carrier groups in the Middle East off the same nominal force of 11 — but that was a surge, sustainable only briefly. This year, Venezuela plus an extended period of carriers-as-coercive-diplomacy produced a 326-day deployment for Ford and 250 days on station off Iran for Abraham Lincoln, against a norm where anything over six months was rare. The mechanism is the load-bearing bit (04:20): long deployments consume airframes, stores and people, and cause missed training and deferred maintenance, so the next work-up takes longer — the cycle slows itself. Relieving Lincoln, which had reports of starvation rations and poor morale, required moving the only Western Pacific carrier out of theatre, a visible overstretch signal for anyone reading US resolve on Taiwan (04:50).

The binding constraint isn't hulls (05:15). Air wings aren't protected by statute: there are nine for 11 carriers, and during Iraq and Afghanistan the system assumed three deployed at a time, with the rest recovering or working up. Four or five at sea breaks that. The same arithmetic applies to the escorting destroyers doing missile defence and to the auxiliaries that sustain the task force.

Vulnerability and cost (06:08). ~$25bn per carrier once you count aviation, munitions and a couple of escorts. A carrier group is hard to sink, but China has spent decades on a family of anti-carrier weapons plus the satellites to cue them. The author's own check is a nice concrete demonstration that the secrecy premise has eroded: commercial satellite imagery confirmed four East Coast flattops were in port — two at Newport News, two at Norfolk (06:50).

Why the law makes it worse (07:10). A legislated floor on carriers means everything unprotected absorbs the cuts when budgets tighten, so the carrier fleet is a net drain on the rest of the Navy — what some in the Pentagon call hollowing out: 11 on paper, three at sea. Underneath that, post-9/11 budget reductions shrank the fleet, closed shipyards, contracted supply chains and let skills lapse, which is the same shortage now showing up as build delays (08:11).

Where it overreaches. "Three at once" is closer to normal rotation arithmetic than the framing admits — roughly a third deployed is the design assumption, not the anomaly. The genuine evidence of decline here is the 326-day deployment, the empty Western Pacific, and nine air wings for 11 hulls. Those are strong; the headline number is the weakest thing in the video.

  • The catapult omission (1 like, detailed): EMALS has been failing reliability tests and is a primary driver of Ford's delay, so "for some reason" is an unforced error. The commenter's leap from that to "the whole video is worthless" is not.
  • A cluster arguing carriers are already obsolete against cheap drones and hypersonic missiles (7, 5, 5, 4, 1…), including the observation that the carriers stood 430–680 miles off Iran. The video raises vulnerability then drops it — this is the debate it declines to have.

The one real idea

Headline inventory numbers understate the problem because not all stored fuel is usable. Tanks and pipelines need a minimum working volume; only barrels above that floor can be drawn down. So the question isn't how many barrels are in storage but how many sit above the floor — and on that measure the US enters peak season with roughly half its normal cushion.

The arithmetic, 03:20–07:30:

  • Total distillate stocks Sep 11, 2026: 107.9M bbl. Three-year September average: 121.3M.
  • At an 85M operating minimum (the 1998 National Petroleum Council "lower operating inventory" for US distillate, 04:50): usable cushion is 22.6M now vs 36.3M normally — 63% of normal.
  • At a 95M minimum: 12.9M vs 26.3M — 49% of normal.
  • Diesel-only ending stocks: 97M vs a 111.3M three-year September average (2022 excluded as a crisis year) — 14.3M short. Diesel is ~90% of distillate, which is why the cushion math runs on total distillate 05:30.

Cause 01:01: refining capacity in the Middle East and Russia is offline from the wars there, so US refineries run near flat out — 96.8% utilization as of Sep 11 — selling into that gap. Distillate exports hit 1.61M bbl/d, up from 1.56M the prior week and above average 03:01. Export demand is being served ahead of the domestic autumn restock. That is the whole mechanism.

Prices 02:05: EIA weekly average on-road diesel was $6.29/gal on Sep 14, an all-time high; AAA showed $6.40 by Sep 17.

Conclusion 08:05: absent federal restrictions on diesel exports, an October–November shortfall is hard to avoid. He is explicit that he does not mean dry pumps — he means materially higher prices and their knock-ons.

Where the confidence outruns the evidence

The 49% figure — the number the video exists to deliver — depends on a 95M bbl operating minimum he obtained by asking Google Gemini 07:05. He says so openly, and his corroboration (total distillate has not closed below 100M since May 2003) is not unreasonable, but it repeats the flaw in the 1998 estimate it replaces: the lowest level observed is not the lowest level workable. And the headline is very sensitive to that guess — 85M gives 63%, 95M gives 49%. An unmeasured quantity is carrying a 14-point swing.

Absent from the analysis: imports (the Northeast is a structural distillate importer), demand destruction from $6.40 diesel itself, and any quantification of the harvest-plus-freight demand increment that the "two weeks" frame rests on. Export restriction is offered as the only lever.

What it does well: every figure is sourced and dated, the obvious objection to his own framing ("97M still sounds like a lot") is raised and answered, and the shortage claim is bounded rather than sensationalized.

What the comments contest

Most of the thread is political recrimination rather than domain correction, but several comments add real content.

  • The "no empty gas stations" caveat is the most substantively disputed claim (33): panic buying produces tiered chain collapse — first-tier stations run dry, their customers double the drain on the next tier, and a region empties. Cited precedent: Hurricane Irma, Florida 2017. Seconded by (33) "there's going to be a run on diesel the moment people realize." The video models supply and demand but not behavior.
  • (17) Diesel and home heating oil are near-identical from a refinery's perspective, so winter tank top-offs are distillate demand sitting inside his own denominator, unmentioned.
  • (229, 38, 30) The SPR has also been drawn down — weekly pumping from ~6M bbl to ~0.4M, effectively exhausted. It holds crude, not distillate, so it is not a direct offset, but it removes the usual release valve his "unless the government acts" framing implies still exists.
  • (24) A truck driver reports two days of work in three weeks and empty truck-stop parking — demand destruction already visible, which cuts against the peak-demand collision premise.
  • (17) An owner-operator's fuel surcharge nets him $1.50/gal, so the squeeze lands on farmers and consumers, not carriers.
  • (8) China cut crude purchases sharply after the war began, muting the shock; a restock would re-tighten. Not addressed.
  • (45) Names the mechanism the video won't: the Strait of Hormuz, closed since March. The video says only "wars in these regions."

If Every Country is having a Debt Crisis... Who's Lending the Money

Skim. Three real ideas — debt is circular, rates rise without anyone refusing to lend, and the fix is career-ending — wrapped in an explainer pitched below this reader's level, with two factual slips.

~4 of 18 min worth watching · 18% fluff · 3,467 → 640 words (5.4:1)

watch on YouTube →

The answer to the title question

Governments borrow from three places: their own citizens, other arms of their own government (Social Security trust funds and equivalents), and foreign governments 01:20. For the US the first two are ~65% of the total; foreign governments hold ~$9.3T 01:45. The UK and France look the same 05:20. So the title mostly dissolves — the lender is the domestic population.

The non-obvious part is how a population carrying $18T of its own debt has $40T to lend 05:40. The three-person diagram at 06:04 is the video's cleanest moment: A borrows 100K from B, later lends 50K to C, C lends 25K to B. Gross debt is 175K; no outside money entered. Global debt is that, with 8 billion participants. Gross stock says nothing about net position.

The reason none of it gets repaid is a single institutional innovation: 18th-century Britain began servicing bonds by issuing new bonds rather than from taxes 04:04. Every state copied it. The video calls this "kind of genius" and never engages the obvious objection.

Why rates are rising without anyone refusing to lend

The load-bearing claim is at 10:30: lenders don't need to stop, they only need to reprice risk. Treasury default risk was treated as ~zero, so rates were low; sustainability doubts add a premium, and on a debt stock this size a couple of points is enormous. Because the network is circular, one broken link propagates, which is why yields rise everywhere at once 10:55.

A second, independent driver is inflation 11:20. Fisher: nominal rate = real rate + expected inflation. G7 inflation peaked at 6–10% post-2020 and still sits at 3–4% against a 2% target, so lenders permanently demand more. Consequence already visible: France burned five prime ministers in two years and forced a budget through without a parliamentary vote 13:07.

What an actual stop looks like: the UK mini-budget, September 2022 13:40. ~£45bn of unfunded tax cuts, markets judged them unpayable, yields spiraled, and it stopped only when Truss resigned. The useful qualifier follows at 14:40 — that was fixable because it was one reversible policy plus a credibility swap. Decades of structural deficits offer no such switch.

The US can't be forced into nominal default because its debt is in a currency it issues 15:09, but printing doesn't erase the debt, it reassigns it: inflation taxes holders of cash, savings and the bonds themselves, spread over time instead of delivered at once 15:40. And it self-feeds — printing raises inflation expectations, which raise rates, which raise the servicing cost 16:09.

The best segment, and it's last

16:40–17:55 is the only part worth watching in full. Nobody fixes this because the payoffs are asymmetric in time: fixing means cutting spending and raising taxes now — concentrated, immediate, career-ending — while ignoring it costs a few basis points a year, diffuse across millions of people and unattributable to any officeholder. Letting debt grow is individually rational for every decision-maker in the chain. This is the video's actual thesis and it's buried at the end.

Where it's wrong

The $300–350T figure is all global debt — household, corporate and government. At 04:40 the video says "governments owe $300 trillion to one another," which is off by roughly 3x; global public debt is ~$100T. The scale visualization built on it 04:45 inherits the error.

"Debt interest is one of, if not the single biggest line item in government budgets" 08:50 is overstated: US net interest is large and near the defense budget, but well below Social Security or health spending.

What the comments contest

  • Map error, 02:12 (19 + 9 + several more): the large red blob is Japan, not China. Multiple independent catches; the video's own cartogram misidentifies its central example.
  • Ponzi framing, 04:10 (40, 24, and echoed widely): commenters name the roll-debt-with-debt mechanism as a Ponzi scheme, legal only for sovereigns. The video presents it approvingly and never addresses the charge.
  • Printing mechanics (8): the Treasury can't "print" — it issues bonds the Fed may purchase. The correction is mechanically right; the appended claim that the Fed is "privately owned" is the standard misreading of its member-bank structure.
  • Title not answered (3): "Who's lending the money... still don't know." One low-vote comment supplies what the video omits — US net international investment position ≈ -$27.5T, China plus Hong Kong ≈ +$6.3T. Net position is the question; the video only ever discusses gross.
  • Banks create deposits (0): the three-person diagram models lending as pure intermediation of existing savings. Commercial bank lending creates deposits, which weakens the diagram's claim that "the money simply came from each other."
  • Sectoral balances (0): if corporate and household sectors both run surpluses, some sector must run the deficit. A real macro objection to treating government debt as a standalone pathology.
  • Nordics (1, 2, 1): Norway and the Nordics contradict "pretty much every Western country."

What it is

Canada accepted an EU invitation to become an "associate member" 00:00; a day later the European Parliament's president floated Australia, with New Zealand alongside 02:05. That is the entire factual basis — everything after is inference.

"Associate member" is not a legal category. Article 49 TEU begins its accession requirements with "must be a European state" 02:40, and nothing anywhere in EU law defines associate membership 03:06. The one expert cited, EU law professor Steve Peers, reads the vacuum as the feature: with nothing defined, both sides can invent the procedure and the content 03:20.

Measured against the normal content of membership — votes on EU policy, the mutual-defence obligation, the customs union, the four freedoms 03:30 — Canada gets approximately none of it 06:50. The customs union is the piece it positively cannot take: membership forbids bilateral side deals, which is exactly the structure Canada walked away from when the Trump administration wanted North America negotiating outward trade as one bloc 07:45. The existing machinery is thin too — ten years of mutual recognition of professional qualifications has produced exactly one recognised qualification architecture 06:30 — and Norway, Iceland and Switzerland, none of them members, already hold more of these rights than Canada will 08:40.

Why it still isn't nothing

The load-bearing claim: this is aimed at investment paralysis, not at tariffs. Firms won't commit capital to US-facing trade for obvious reasons, and won't commit it to alternatives either, in case the US reverts and they get outcompeted by firms that sat still 10:30. A title that is politically very hard to revoke is a cheap way to signal that trans-Atlantic terms will hold long enough to underwrite a twenty-year asset. On this reading the emptiness of the membership is not a defect — the point was never the rights, it was the difficulty of walking it back.

The underlying fit is unglamorous and real: the EU stopped buying Russian energy and raw materials, Canada stopped selling them to the US 09:40. The corroborating tell is that Washington is visibly furious about something it is simultaneously calling a marketing stunt 09:00.

The part with teeth

Joint defence procurement 11:28. A pooled hardware budget that largely excludes American contractors in favour of EU, Canadian and potentially Australian and NZ suppliers — and hardware generates decades of maintenance and resupply contracts with its original manufacturer, so buying French jets binds Canada to France for the airframe's service life 12:31. That is a far longer commitment than any paper membership, and it defunds firms that are among the loudest lobbyists in Washington. The US asked NATO members to spend more on defence; they are, elsewhere 13:34.

Downstream: less US leverage in the North American renegotiation now that Canada visibly has an option 13:45; less leverage with the EU, where energy was central to the largest bilateral trade relationship in the world 14:35; some further marginal pressure on the dollar, asserted rather than argued 14:45. One speculative upside — if Canada, Australia and NZ align on EU product standards for data privacy and AI, big tech loses the option of treating Europe as a walled-off compliance zone 15:00. Process problem: many member states weren't consulted before the invitation went out 15:39, which reads both as Brussels being serious and as an opening for spite-driven pushback.

Where it's wrong

The framing premise — that this is an extreme long-term fix for a problem that "probably won't be around in two years" 01:30, 10:15 — is stated twice and argued zero times. It also contradicts the video's own best argument: investment paralysis only resolves if counterparties believe the risk is durable. If the two-year claim were true, the membership would accomplish nothing.

The mechanic worth the click

08:06 — the crossover point: the balance at which one average year of market returns equals one year of your contributions. The video gives it as a rule of thumb — annual contribution × 14 — without saying what the 14 is. It is just 1 ÷ expected return. Crossover balance = annual contribution ÷ return rate, so ×14.3 at 7% and ×12.5 at 8% (the description quietly corrects the video to the 8% figure). At $1,000/month that lands at ~$171,000, roughly ten years in. $500/month → $86,000; $2,000/month → $343,000.

Everything else in the milestone ladder is that same ratio rendered at different balances. All figures assume $1,000/month invested from day one — which, usefully, is exactly the median earner he cites saving 20% ($458/paycheck × 26 ≈ $11,900/yr), so the timelines are internally coherent:

| Balance | Time at $1,000/mo | Your money / market's | Returns vs. contributions | |---|---|---|---| | $1,000 | — | — | ~$80/yr 00:00 | | $10,000 | 10 months | $9,700 / $287 | negligible 02:02 | | $50,000 | 3y 8m | $43,296 / $6,700 | ~1/3 03:02 | | $100,000 | 77 months | $77k / $23k | ~2/3 05:03 | | $171,000 | ~10 years | — | 1:1 — crossover 08:06 | | $250,000 | — | $147k / $103k | ~1.7× 11:06 | | $500,000 | — | — | ~3× 12:06 | | $1,000,000 | ~27 years | $330k / $670k | ~6× 14:06 |

Two derived facts he does state and that do the real work: the second $100K arrives ~33% faster than the first (~51 vs. 77 months) 05:03, and 2/3 of the final million is money you never worked for 14:06.

The second real claim

07:30 — the cost of coasting. Hit $100K and keep investing $1,000/month: a million in ~19 years. Hit $100K and stop: ~29 years. Ten years of your life for the decision to take your foot off the gas at the exact moment compounding starts paying.

Two things explained well

13:10 — sequence-of-returns risk, in about thirty seconds and without jargon: withdrawing during a crash means selling shares at a discount, and the shares you sold aren't there to recover when the market does. Best teaching in the video.

10:06 — fees. A 1% advisory fee is $2,500/yr at $250K (≈21% of what you're contributing) and $10,000/yr at $1M; because the fee never compounds, 1% over 30 years leaves you roughly a quarter poorer. That last figure checks out (1.07³⁰ vs. 1.06³⁰ = 24.5%).

What "changes" at each level, compressed

Below $10K, survival makes your decisions — you take the first offer, you stay in the bad apartment 01:02. $10K buys a buffer against an $1,800 car repair and enough leverage to negotiate. $50K funds a 3–6 month career exit only if it's liquid — a 401(k) balance doesn't buy optionality 03:02. $250K enables Coast FIRE: $250K at 35, never another dollar, is ~$2.5M at 65; $500K at 45 is ~$2.3M 11:06. At $1M, 4% withdrawals ($40K) plus two Social Security checks (~$24K each) give a household ~$88K/yr against a median working household's ~$80K 14:06.

Benchmarks cited: median net worth $39K under 35, $193K overall (incl. home equity), $658K at the 75th percentile, $1.9M at the top 10%; only 3–5% of retirees hold $1M+ in a retirement account (Fed SCF) 15:06.

The one non-numeric claim, and the best one: almost nothing changes psychologically at $1M, and the feeling fades within weeks 15:06. Goalposts move because people only ever compare upward.

Where it's sloppy

The video quotes 8% returns throughout but computes every timeline and the ×14 crossover rule at 7% — including "$500K earns $35,000/yr," which is 7%, not 8%. Minor, but it's the whole spine of the arithmetic.

Worse: it applies 8% market returns to the $1,000 and $10,000 milestones, which it simultaneously defines as emergency money. An emergency fund belongs in cash. Treating your insurance as invested capital is the one actively bad idea here.

The "absolute drawdowns feel bigger as you grow" point is made three separate times (06:04, 12:06, 15:06) in identical form.

The load-bearing claim

China's rare-earth monopoly is not geological. The monopoly is in separation: the step everyone else declined to do because it is chemically brutal and environmentally filthy. China took it, then spent two decades converting that one chokepoint into control of the whole downstream chain. On 4 April 2025 it used that control coercively for the first time, which is the event the video is actually about.

The stakes are set in the first minute: 96 cents of every dollar flowing through the rare-earth industry ends in magnets (03:45), and there is no substitute at the same size and weight. An EV motor holds ~12 lb of magnet; every US missile at least twelve (00:30). China controls 94% of magnet rare earths — and, as it turns out, a number that matters more.

Why separation is the moat

Adjacent lanthanides are chemically near-identical — same size, same bonding behaviour, same grip on other atoms (08:20). No single test sorts them. You run dozens of stages, hundreds of times, each pass nudging one twin marginally left and the other marginally right. Among mined metals they are the hardest family to separate from each other: most steps, most chemicals, most time. The same geochemistry that clumps them into findable deposits is what makes them inseparable once found.

The waste is the other half. At Mountain Pass, California — the richest deposit ever worked, where one scoop is 90% ordinary rock and only the neodymium really pays (05:00) — process water went 14 miles down a pipeline to evaporate in the desert, fully permitted. Over 12 years the line ruptured 60+ times, scaled internally with thorium the purification didn't remove, spilling close to a million gallons (10:40). Lawsuits and fines effectively ended US rare-earth processing.

Bayan Obo in Inner Mongolia does the identical thing into a mile-square open tailings pond (11:20). The difference is legal, not technical: rare earths are designated critical, so a suit wouldn't be lost — it wouldn't be heard (12:03). That asymmetry, not labour cost, is the margin.

The four-step playbook

The video's best section (14:25–17:05), and it generalises to aluminium, solar, steel, ships.

1. Build at home on state money. State banks lend far below Western cost; the party allocates land; the backer doesn't need a return next quarter. Sell to everybody — volume buys scale and a trained workforce. 2. Shut the door. Export quotas or prohibitive taxes — this landed in late 2010. Prices outside China roughly quadrupled; inside, they didn't move. Two prices for the same material. 3. Wait. This is the step that wins. Foreign magnet and motor makers get undercut by domestic rivals buying cheap. Chinese customers start demanding tighter specs, so Chinese separators climb the learning curve. Foreign firms, unable to buy half-finished metal, outsource the magnet itself — handing over drawings, quantities, recipes. Nobody stole the know-how; it was paid for and delivered. An iPhone is ~$9 of raw material selling for ~120× that (16:20): the point of closing the door is to force your own economy up to where the money is. 4. Open it again. Trade cases take 3–5 years and China usually loses, but the clock is the product — competitors bleed out first. High prices also tempt new mines in Canada, Australia and the US, so reopening floods supply below what a newcomer can dig rock for, and they fail. Domestic survivors then merge into two state champions: China Northern Rare Earth Group and China Rare Earth Group.
